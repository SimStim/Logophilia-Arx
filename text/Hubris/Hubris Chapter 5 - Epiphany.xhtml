Subject: Expansion Debate
Location: Qhennth, Grthnnar, Vrhlath’k Shrine
Date: 3.47.ba.70−
Protagonists: Zhyr’kael Vrosyth, Chronarch of the Veils; Vrylhēn Ny’raet, Chronarch of the Veils; Thryshak Keldruhn, Soulkeeper of the Infinite Weave; Brhylth Korrinath, Meldshaper of the Iron Mind; Qyrlthyn Vrzzokh, Judicator of the Celestial Accord

The chamber thrummed with the collective focus of its distinguished attendees, the low undertone resonating from the walls as holographic projections danced across the air: star systems, resource trajectories, and speculative galactic expansion routes swirling in midair. The debate had spanned dozens of periastra, and patience was beginning to fray. The stakes demanded resolution.

Should their species remain bound to their home system, or should they embark on an ambitious expansion into the galaxy?

Zhyr’kael Vrosyth, the leading expert on predictive systems, rose first. His body, a shimmering lattice of refracted light, spoke to their mastery over cosmic foresight, each movement rippling with precision. “Let us begin with the core fact: Expansion is survival,” he stated, his voice resonating with the certainty of years spent analyzing the stars.

“Every model we have run indicates that a single catastrophic event—a nova, a galactic collision, or even internal destabilization—could annihilate our species. We cannot afford the risk of being confined to a single system. Diversification across the galaxy mitigates this existential threat.”

Thryshak Keldruhn, renowned for his profound philosophical insights, countered immediately. His voice was steady, clinical, his tone adamant. “You argue from fear, Vrosyth. Catastrophes are low-probability events over the timescales we calculate. But concentration, in contrast, offers undeniable advantages: knowledge transfer, cohesion, resource conservation. These benefits outweigh a nebulous, distant risk.”

Vrosyth gestured sharply toward the holographic simulations, the shifting star systems and resource trajectories. “You call it fear? I call it prudence. A civilization confined to a single system is fragile by nature. One errant gamma-ray burst, a failure of planetary infrastructure, and we are no more. Are you truly willing to gamble the entirety of our species on the precarious gamble of remaining confined?”

Brhylth Korrinath, a formidable expert in global strategy, interjected, his voice low but firm. “Expansion carries its own risks, Vrosyth. Think of the strain on resources, the logistics of establishing colonies in far-flung regions of the galaxy. Communication delays across vast distances would cripple coordinated action. Isolated outposts with diminishing effectiveness, prone to divergence in technology and thought. That is the reality of expansion. Fragmentation, by its very nature, weakens.”

Others in the chamber murmured their agreement, their discontent with Vrosyth’s position palpable in the air. Korrinath pressed on, his eyes fixed firmly on the Chronarch. “We value unity, not disarray. We value control, not disorder. An empire that spans light-years is an empire that can never act as one.”

Keldruhn leaned forward, his gaze unwavering, eyes intense. “Unity is not merely an ideal, it is but the foundation of our survival. The preservation of knowledge, the legacy of our species, depends on our cohesion. Fragmentation leads only to decay. And decay leads to extinction.”

Vrosyth’s surface dimmed, the deepening shadows signaling his growing frustration. “And what happens when resources here dwindle?” he asked, his voice cutting through the chamber. “What happens when we exhaust the raw materials necessary for our continued advancement? Without expansion, we face stagnation.”

Vrylhēn Ny’raet, silent until now, raised a slender hand, signaling for permission to speak. He was cool, composed. “We must indeed consider innovation. But while I validate and respect the concern for stagnation, our data probes span vast distances. They provide a steady influx of new knowledge without the need for physical expansion. Phenomena from systems dozens of light-years away have already supplied valuable insights. Expansion, in this case, may not be necessary to avoid intellectual stagnation.”

Korrinath made a slow, deliberate gesture of agreement, his expression thoughtful. “Exactly. Our probes allow us to explore the galaxy without risking our unity. Why jeopardize the integrity of our civilization by scattering ourselves when the universe is delivering its secrets to us, piece by piece?”

Vrosyth’s irritation flared. “Probes are limited, Korrinath. They observe, but they do not act. Colonization offers opportunities for new experimental conditions: real-world variables that could lead to breakthroughs beyond anything we can achieve here.”

Keldruhn’s voice sharpened, his patience thinning. “And at what cost, Vrosyth? Expansion would demand the sacrifice of vast resources. Our path of progress is steep. Diverting resources to colonization would slow advancements in our home system, wasting doz’qas on fragile outposts. Why sacrifice everything we’ve built here on a gamble for an uncertain future?”

The chamber fell into a drawn-out silence, the density of the arguments immersing everyone present. All eyes turned toward the Celestial Accord, the final arbiter of the Qhennthyk’s future.

Qyrlthyn Vrzzokh, rose. His form was larger and more intricate than any of the others present, an embodiment of authority and wisdom. As he spoke, his voice carried the weight of doz’qas of decision-making.

“The question before us,” Vrzzokh said, his voice resonating through the chamber, “is not one of survival alone. It is a question of purpose.” His eyes turned toward the projections of the galaxy, his delicate surface skin catching the light in a shimmering reflection. “Does our survival depend on scattering ourselves among the stars? Or does it depend on preserving what we have here, within our cradle?”

There was a gravity to his words, a force that calmed even the soft whisper of the chamber. Vrzzokh continued, his presence commanding attention. “Unity is our strength. Knowledge is our inheritance. To expand now is to risk the fragmentation of both. We have seen through our probes that the galaxy offers us data, and data is sufficient for now. We do not need to wander the stars to learn.”

He turned his gaze back to the projections, contemplating the paths that could stretch out before them. “Our existence is fragile, yes. But dispersing ourselves would not strengthen it; it would strain it. Our system is a cradle, one that has nurtured us to this point, and here we will stay. We will advance here, within the confines of our system, until such a time as expansion is unavoidable.”

Vrzzokh raised a hand, commanding silence before Vrosyth could protest. “The risks of isolation,” he said, his voice sharp and final, “will be mitigated. We will redouble our efforts to protect this system. We will create fail-safes. And our probes will continue to expand our reach without diluting our strength.”

A long pause followed. The members of the Accord were left in contemplation, their thoughts a mixture of uncertainty and reluctant agreement.

The chamber rang with the Judicator’s final words, his voice carrying across the vast space like a ringing bell, sealing the decision: “Our survival lies not in scattering our essence, but in perfecting it. Let the stars call to us; for now, we remain steadfast in our sanctuary. The galaxy will wait for us.”

There was no room for further debate as the words settled, resonating with an undeniable ruling. The Qhennthyk, though capable of reaching for the galaxy, chose instead to stay close to their home and to their purpose.

The decision had been made with the measured intention of preservation.

Queneth Ovrin completed his inspection of the external maintenance panel and paused, his eyes drifting to the pale expanse extending beyond the dome. The horizon stretched into eternity, a barren canvas of muted crimson, broken only by the faint outlines of distant craters. Life on Mars was a precarious dance between fragility and resilience, one they had mastered through relentless precision. The colony, small but tough, had been established with one goal in mind: survival.

Queneth Ovrin completed his inspection of the external maintenance panel.

Surviving Mars was a matter of careful calculation. The fully automated greenhouses yielded an abundance of fresh produce, rendering emergency rations a rare necessity. The systems governing oxygen, water, and food were intricately linked, each computerized to reduce the margin of error to near zero. The colony was designed to run with minimal human oversight.

The low whine of machinery and the rhythmic whir of life support systems formed a constant, mechanical lullaby, an unchanging symphony of continuity. Queneth glanced at his wrist console: oxygen levels steady, the greenhouse’s water reclamation stable overnight, and the energy grid operating well above capacity. A quick check of the temperature and wind speed confirmed a typical Martian morning. Satisfied, he moved on.

Work here was purely practical: no room for distractions, no margin for error. Martian dust, thick and gummy, stuck stubbornly to the base of the dome like a siege that could never breach the walls. The colony’s airlocks and seals held fast: fortresses against the planet’s obstinate harshness. The dust didn’t creep in, not unless there was a failure in the airlock systems or someone was careless.

Mars’s deadliest threats weren’t dramatic; they were insidious. A missed calculation, a faulty sensor, or a slight fluctuation in oxygen levels could unravel their fragile existence, one unnoticed detail at a time. Things could break down when you weren’t paying attention. And so, they remained wary. Mars had taught them that survival demanded constant vigilance, never the luxury of complacency.

With his round complete, Queneth made his way back to the common area, the promise of breakfast guiding him through the sterile corridors. The place buzzed with subdued energy, a sanctuary of shared warmth against the vast, unfeeling Martian desert. Familiar faces, the ones who had become his surrogate family, were scattered around the room. Quiet chatter mingled with the rumble of machines: updates on greenhouse yields, minor repairs, and snippets of news from the colony’s shared network.

Queneth joined the queue at the food dispenser, his foot tapping a quiet rhythm of impatience. The line stretched longer than usual, the throb of machinery underscoring the muted mumbles of colonists. Complaints drifted through the air, something about a glitch in the ration system earlier that morning. Ahead, two technicians debated mineral quotas, their voices rising as they scrutinized numbers and allocation plans. It was another day on Mars: routine, demanding, and utterly essential.

Tray in hand, Queneth weaved through the bustling common area, the faint aroma of rehydrated stew and synthetic protein crackers blending with the echo of conversation. He spotted a seat in the corner, squeezed between two engineers locked in a heated game of holochess.

Queneth eased into his seat and slipped out his datapad, eager for a fleeting connection to Earth, a brief escape from the monotony of Martian life. A soft chime broke his focus, drawing his gaze to the far wall. The screen, usually alive with weather updates and colony statistics, flickered before plunging into darkness. A hush rippled through the room. The hall’s chatter quieted as an unfamiliar face appeared on the display.

It was the image of a middle-aged man, his weathered face carved with resolve. When he spoke, his voice carried a disquieting calm that sent a ripple of unease through Queneth’s chest.

“Citizens, I speak not as a symbol, but as bearer of truth.”

A heavy silence blanketed the onlookers. Across the table, Queneth leaned forward, his spoon hovering mid-air, forgotten.

“An entity, alien in origin—a fleet, perhaps—has breached our solar system.”

The words crackled through the air like static, leaving an uneasy charge in their wake. Some colonists exchanged nervous glances, while others sat motionless, their eyes fixed on the screen.

Dikshael continued, his tone unwavering. “Hostile intent. A power that eclipses all we command.”

A chill prickled the back of Queneth’s neck. His heart clawed at his ribs, frantic as a caged thing, as the words echoed in his mind. Aliens? Hostile ones? The idea was absurd, the stuff of The Pitch Science Fiction anthologies he’d devoured as a kid. And yet, the certainty in Dikshael’s voice chewed at him.

The broadcast had cut to black, leaving the room in a vacuum of sound. For a fleeting moment, the only noise was the faint buzz of the life-support systems. Then, chaos erupted.

“This gotta be a scherz, ja?” a voice from the far end of the hall rang out, sharp and incredulous, punctuated by the slam of a hand on plasteel.

“No way Earth keeps this hush-hush if it’s echt!” another colonist argued, his voice quivered with doubt. Queneth recognized him as Phrenk from occasionally shared shifts on the rotating schedule ... though he had always considered Phrenk a measured and calm person.

He nodded absently, the questions swirling in his mind. Turning to the engineers at the holochess deck, he asked, “So, what machst du of it? Could it really be echt, or what?”

One of them shrugged, his expression grim. “Nix data shown ... no orbits, no specs, nix. All geplapper,” he muttered, his tone laconic and skeptical.

“But why does Earth let some dummkopf blab this out?” Queneth pressed. “Shouldn’t this come straight from the World Council, oder?”

“Maybe they don’t want folks losin’ their käse,” the other engineer suggested, though his smirk betrayed his own unease. “Or maybe the Earth bigwigs are too busy flippin’ their lids to make it official, ach!” His laughter came in strange staccato bursts, so unnatural that Queneth couldn’t tell if it was deliberate or just nerves.

He stayed quiet, his thoughts churning. The idea of an alien presence, no matter how far away, felt both unreal and disturbingly close. The impact of it settled on him, massive and inescapable. If it was true, what did it mean for them, here on Mars?

Across the hall, heated debates broke out. The space fractured into frenzied arguments. Some scoffed at the broadcast, calling it propaganda or an elaborate hoax. Others, their voices tinged with urgency, argued that even a sliver of truth demanded preparation.

Queneth caught fragments of conversations as he made his way to his bunk in the evening:

“Even if it’s echt, why could they bother with Mars? We are no target, ja.”

“No target?” someone shot back. “You think they stop at Earth? Wach auf, freund. You could talk the bark off a tree.”

“We are sittin’ enten out here,” another muttered. “No defenses, no backup ... nix.”

That night, sleep eluded Queneth. He lay in the dim glow of his bunk’s control panel, his mind a whirl of ragged conversations and unanswered questions. The thin Martian air felt harsher than usual, cramping his chest. He turned onto his side, then back again, but the unease lingered, stubborn and suffocating.

As he stared at the dull ceiling of his quarters, Queneth couldn’t shake the feeling that the broadcast had been the beginning of something, not the end. Whatever came next, Mars would not remain untouched.

The following day, rumors crackled through the colony like static electricity. Some swore they’d seen strange lights flickering above the horizon, though the dusty Martian skies offered no proof. Rumors spread feverishly, weaving unease into the fabric of daily tasks. Others turned to Earth’s public feeds, hunting for scraps of information, but what they found was sparse, vague ... ominous in its absence.

As evening fell, clarity arrived like a thunderclap—and with it, fear. The common area filled with colonists, their conversations muted, discomfort thick in the air. All eyes turned to the central display as it flickered to life. Prime Arbiter Thalexa Solenne’s face appeared, her expression composed but her eyes sharp, as if bracing for the storm she was about to unleash.

She spoke with practiced gravitas: “Citizens of Sol, the time for conjecture is concluded. We confront an undeniable reality: an alien armada has breached our solar boundaries. Its trajectory converges upon Earth.”

A stunned silence settled over the room.

Then came the footage. Cities burned in riots, their streets overflowing with desperate crowds. Religious extremists shouted into megaphones, proclaiming divine wrath, their voices drowned out by the chaos. Military convoys rumbled through smoke-filled streets, soldiers struggling to hold back the rising tide of panic. Each image felt like a hammer blow, and the Martians watched, transfixed, as the foundations of Earth’s order seemed to crumble.

A quiet voice broke the stillness: “So, it’s echt, ja.” That voice belonged to his immediate superior, a stoic man with deep insight.

The words lingered, sharp as cryon. Queneth felt an icy shiver crawl up his spine. His stomach churned, his pulse quickening as the enormity of the announcement overwhelmed his consciousness. Around the room, the jitters erupted into muffled exchanges, nervous glances, and disjointed debates.

The confirmation of the alien fleet rippled through the colony, carried from bunk to corridor, growing darker and more distorted with each retelling. The tension reached its breaking point. Colonists clustered in tight groups, their faces carved with worry and exhaustion. The resonance of the common area, once a backdrop for idle chatter, now bristled with frantic energy.

Fragments of conversation reverberated through the space, snatched pieces of anxiety and speculation:

“What if they come here?”

“They waste no time on Mars. Earth is their mark, no?”

“And when Earth falls ... what comes then for us?”

The room had become unrecognizable. The quiet murmur of daily life was replaced by overlapping voices as colonists huddled in circles or paced the floor in restless loops. Even the omnipresent whir of machinery seemed faint beneath the rising tide of human jitters.

Queneth found himself pulled into one of the larger groups, drawn by the magnetic force of collective panic. The air vibrated with a dissonance of ideas thrown like lifelines, fears voiced in trembling tones, and half-formed plans spat out in frustration.

He wanted to speak, to cut through the noise with a question or a suggestion, but his words lodged in his throat. Instead, he stood quietly, his mind racing to keep up with the torrent of voices around him. The impact of the news hit him with force like a leaden shroud, and in the swirl of chaos, one thought rose above the rest: What if this is just the beginning?

“We must stay and hold!” a young woman in a fiery-red hazmat coverall reserved for security personnel shouted, her voice cutting through the clamor. “If we scatter, we stand even weaker.”

“Hold with what?” the man across her retorted, his tone sharp with irritation. “This is not Earth, we have no weapons, and we cannot dig in!”

“What if they take no notice of us?” the woman next to Queneth suggested, her voice charged with fragile hope. “Mars is small, far-off. Maybe they pass by us.” When Queneth turned to her, he couldn’t help but notice the concern in her eyes and the trembling of her clasped fingers.

“And if they don’t?” Queneth blurted out before he could stop himself. The group focused on him, their looks burdensome with expectation. He hesitated, feeling the difficulty to explain, then forced himself to continue. “If they are hostile and choose to come here, we have no time to get away from them. We must act now ... before it’s too late, ja.”

His words settled like dust after a storm.

Gradually, the chaos shifted. The debates grew more measured, the arguments sharper, the panic tempered by a grim determination. From the disorganized discussions, a plan began to take shape, weak but tangible.

“We scatter,” one of the engineers finally said, echoing Queneth’s earlier suggestion. Her voice was steady, resolute. “Every space-worthy ship we have we send out, spread across the system. Scattered, we are harder to strike as one foe, no?”

“And the colony?” the woman in the security uniform interjected, her tone of voice tinged with doubt.

“We leave it whole,” the engineer replied without hesitation. “We set the systems to run alone, make it look like an empty outpost. When they come, maybe they waste time looking at nothing.”

“And the sensors? They give us away, no?” Queneth asked, worry defining his voice.

“Set them on passive observation mode,” the engineer replied without missing a beat. “No active beam, no signals to give us away, ja? When they come near, the sensors can still give us some knowledge about them.”

The plan wasn’t perfect, but it was all they had. A sense of determination settled over the group, despite the glaring risks.

With the outline of their strategy in place, the colonists moved quickly, dividing into teams. Each person had a role to play, and each task was critical. The significance of the situation was palpable, but there was no turning back now.

Apantesis, day 4.

The next day, word spread that data packets from Earth had arrived overnight, detailing the alien fleet’s subtle but significant shift in trajectory. Panic flared and swelled through the colony, waning only when it became clear that the fleet’s velocity had not changed. The message was unmistakable regardless: they had just enough time to carry out their plans ... and only if they stayed focused, following a relentless schedule.

Colony maintenance was kept to a bare minimum. Engineers focused solely on retrofitting the ships, converting cargo holds to carry passengers, and ensuring the vessels were space-worthy. Technicians recalibrated the colony’s automated systems, setting the sensors to passive data retrieval. Logistics teams scurried to gather oxygen tanks, water filtration units, food rations, and other supplies, while medics packed emergency kits, preparing for the cramped, uncertain journey ahead.

Queneth joined a logistics team, hauling crates of supplies to the ships. The work was grueling, but it gave him purpose, a way to keep the rising dread at bay. The security woman in the fiery-red hazmat suit was also part of his team, and he learned her name was Erika. Though he didn’t like her any better for it....

Hours slipped by in a blur of movement. The colony, usually quiet and orderly, had transformed into a chaotic flurry of activity. Families packed up their few belongings, children clinging to toys and keepsakes. The ships were steadily reshaped, their cargo holds finally turned into makeshift living quarters.

As night fell, the colonists gathered one last time in the common area. Exhaustion imprinted on their faces, but beneath it lay a quiet determination.

“This is it,” said one of the engineers Queneth had met the other day playing holochess, his voice quietly reflecting the burden they all felt. “Done all we can.”

Queneth scanned the room, taking in the mixture of fear and resolve in every expression. There was a strange sense of unity in the air, a shared purpose that bound them together, for better or worse.

“We can make it,” he said quietly, more to himself than anyone else. “We must.”

Apantesis, day 5.

In the early hours of the morning, the common area reverberated with nervous energy. Edginess stiffened the air, weighing down on everyone around. Conversations collided as the colonists debated their next move anew. There were no leaders here, no one to make the final call, only the clamor of voices, each offering their own piece of the uncertain future.

“We cannot leave too soon, oder?” It was Erika arguing again, her voice trembling despite her best effort to remain composed. “We have nicht enough supplies to wander all over. Time it wrong, and we starve before we even figure what’s goin’ on.” It was obvious that she was still not convinced by the whole plan.

“And wait too long, then we do not maybe get the chance at all, eh?” someone retorted from the back. “What if the aliens see our liftoff? See movement, and take us out before orbit?”

The room erupted in a chatter of voices, some low and hesitant, others raised and frantic, all laced with anxiety. Mars had taught them to survive in isolation and hardship, but facing an unknown alien threat was an entirely different kind of terror. Even with their preparations in place the day before, the crushing fear of making the wrong choice was paralyzing.

Queneth shifted uncomfortably in his seat near the edge of the room. He rarely spoke during these discussions, but the longer the debate dragged on, the more his unease tightened in his chest. The same arguments were beginning to repeat themselves.

“We overthink this too much, leute,” he said finally, his voice slicing through the ruckus. Heads snapped in his direction. For a moment, he wished he could shrink back into his chair, but he pressed on. “We don’t know with precision where the aliens are, or when they kommen. Nix solid info. But we know: when we stay, we’re sittin’ enten. Better to risk now, when we still steer our own ship, ja? Later we might as well piss into the wind, no?”

The extended silence falling over the room wasn’t comforting. If anything, it felt like the calm before something worse. But it was at least a reprieve from the noise.

Mayla, a retired astrophysicist and respected community member, leaned forward, her look steady, eyes piercing. “Queneth has recht. We done all we can. Ships are ready, supplies are packed, scatter-plan is set. Wait longer, and margin for error shrinks to nix.”

“Leave now, and we run maybe out of resources.” It was Erika voicing her concerns again, nervously. “If it is still safe here, we waste time, waste fuel, waste food when we go too early.”

“We monitor as we go, from safe abstand,” the astrophysicist replied, her voice calm but firm. “When it’s clear, small groups can always come back and resupply. But sit here and wait? That’s cheaper than dirt and half as useful. That’s just hopin’ the aliens send us an invite.”

The murmurs that followed were quieter, more contemplative. The discomfort was still palpable, but something had shifted. Slowly, the arguments began to align. The decision had been made: they would leave soon, maybe not immediately, but within the next few hours, just enough time to run final checks on the ships and the computerized systems they would leave behind.

Queneth exhaled, feeling a flicker of relief. At least the uncertainty was over. The direction was set.

As the colony scrambled to prepare for liftoff, the atmosphere adjusted. Fear still filled the air, but now it was mixed with determination. People moved with purpose, checking provisions, running diagnostics, double-checking navigation systems. The ships, hastily retrofitted cargo haulers, were cramped and bare. They were nothing like the vessels they would have preferred, but they were all they had.

When the time came to board, there was no ceremony, no grand farewell. Families clung to each other in quiet conversations, exchanging vague, uncertain words. Queneth filed into his assigned vessel, the Probably Going to Work, finding a narrow seat by a porthole. Outside, the colony’s structures loomed in the distance, their outlines sharp against the endless rust of the Martian landscape.

As the engines roared to life, Queneth felt the vibration deep in his chest: a low, rumbling tremor that seemed to mirror the angst and hope twisting inside him. The ship lifted slowly, the copper dust of Mars swirling beneath her. Through the porthole, he caught one last, protracted glimpse of the colony, the intimate structures shrinking in the distance before they vanished completely.

After reaching escape velocity, the ship’s engines purred softly, a gentle and more comforting rumble than a cat could ever produce, promising safe passage to all who called her home.

In orbit, the ships separated, each veering off into her own path. The plan was simple: scatter, stay small. Maybe, if they appeared insignificant enough, they’d remain unnoticed.

Queneth stared out at the vast, empty expanse of space, his mind racing. Mars was now just a dot behind them, its scarlet surface slowly disappearing into the black. Somewhere out there, Earth would also be facing an unimaginable threat soon.... And here they were, a tiny group of survivors, trying to remain invisible.

An inquiry next to him imploded his contemplation. “You think we can make it?”

Queneth hesitated before responding, his voice a faint breath against the drone of the ship. “I don’t know,” he admitted, the words more brooding than he expected. “But at least we try.”

The ship thrummed steadily around them as they drifted into the unknown, their feelings packed with both hope and uncertainty. And as Mars faded completely into the dark, it felt as if they were all falling into an endless void, not just of space, but of their own future.

A few hours after leaving Mars, the eerie atmosphere that had settled over the scattered fleet was abruptly shattered by an incoming transmission from the colony’s automated monitoring systems. Most of the colonists had finally begun to rest, exhausted by the frantic preparations and the stress of their escape. But the sharp, urgent tone accompanying the incoming data snatched them from their stupor.

Queneth shot upright in his seat, twisting toward the screen displaying the incoming reports. The feed was a blur of text, graphs, and numbers scrolling too quickly for him to make sense of. Around him, others in the cramped cabin leaned forward, craning their necks toward the small terminal, their voices low with growing concern.

“Was is this about, then?” someone asked, their voice thick with anxiety.

The pilot, Jeyne Myrrick, a grizzled woman with a permanent scowl carved into her face, glanced back briefly, her brow furrowed. “Nicht sure yet. Colony sensors pick up somethin’ big.”

In another ship, the Sorry About the Dust, Kaelen Dreston, one of the colony’s senior engineers, had taken over the data monitoring. His face, pale and tense, was lit only by the dim glow of his terminal. “Das kann nicht stimmen,” he muttered, running the numbers again, but the readings remained unchanged. He tapped into the fleet’s intercom, his voice cracking slightly as he addressed the whole flotilla.

“We register marsquakes, extrem stark,” Kaelen’s voice crackled over the intercom. “Over sechs magnitude. Biggest ever before was fünf-point-sechs. This here ... somethin’ else.”

He paused, swallowing hard before continuing. “And das schlimmste: cannot find the epicenter. Not from known fault lines, not from tectonic shift. Was makes them ... ist not natural.”

A ripple of dread passed through the fleet as the message was relayed between ships. Queneth’s stomach tightened. He could see it reflected in the faces around him, the same thought forming in everyone’s mind.

“Is the aliens,” someone declared, the words hanging in the air like a curse.

Kaelen’s voice came through again, his tone grim, confirming what they were all beginning to suspect. “Quakes not random. Data shows pattern ... grows steady, gets schlimmer fast. Only logical explanation says: alien fleet messin’ mit local spacetime. Mars feels it first.”

The words settled violently in the air. The aliens were shattering the very fabric of the Martian environment, even from a distance.

From transmitted imagery, the colonists watched as the damage began to unfold on the surface of their home. The familiar domes of the colony, once sturdy against Mars’s harsh conditions, now splintered like brittle bones. Tremors had hemorrhaged oxygen tanks into the dust, and the computerized systems reported rapid atmospheric loss. Resources stored in underground depots were exposed, scattered like debris across the barren landscape.

Queneth rested his forehead against the cold cryon of the porthole, his eyes staring into a dark abyss. He tried to imagine where Mars was, but the emptiness of space only stared back at him.

The ships drifted in uneasy silence, their occupants glued to the latest data pouring in from Mars and the surrounding space. The colony’s systems that were still functioning sent bursts of telemetry about the intensifying tremors. With no answers of their own, the Martian colonists forwarded everything to Earth, hoping that someone there could make sense of it.

“Quakes let up yet?” someone asked over the fleet’s intercom.

Kaelen’s reply was grim. “Nein. Still grows in magnitude.”

A young colonist, sitting near Queneth, gripped the edge of his seat, his voice trembling. “If they do this to Mars ... was happens to Earth? To mankind?”

No one had an answer.

The Deep Analysis Room buzzed with low murmurs as a group of leading scientists huddled around a central console, their faces exhibiting disbelief and resignation. Holographic projections of data streams, gravitational field simulations, and dark-matter extraction models floated above them, casting their features in a cold, ghostly blue light.

“It’s unbelievable,” muttered Nyvara, running a hand through her graying hair. “But the calculations are sound. The energy fluctuations match exactly with what we’ve theorized. Dark-matter manipulation on this scale...”

“... is catastrophic,” Kaelith finished, his voice barely audible, a shadow of disbelief hanging over every word. The physicist with a track record of breakthroughs in dark-matter research gestured toward the trembling holograms of Earth and Mars. “We’ve never seen anything like this.
