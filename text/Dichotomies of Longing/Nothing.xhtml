The alarm at six in the morning tears through the bedroom's stillness. Ed rises, dresses in the half-dark, and performs the motions of a life that is coming apart at the seams. Returning home, his son Marcus sits at the kitchen table, his food untouched, his gaze fixed on a point somewhere beyond the room. When Ed asks about his day, about his thoughts, about anything at all, the answer is a single word, delivered without inflection.

The word becomes a wall, rising higher each day. Ed watches his son retreat into a silence that feels less like peace and more like a slow disappearance. The house itself reflects the erosion: toys abandoned mid-story, dust settling on the spines of books that once held a boy's bright attention. And in the corner of the room, Sharon, his wife, scrolls through the curated world of her social feed, her laughter bright and easy, her gaze never quite landing on the child who stands motionless, watching her.

A story about what it costs a child to go unheard, and what it costs a father to finally listen.

Nothing

The alarm at six in the morning tore through the bedroom’s stillness, thin and insistent. Ed groaned, his hand fumbling across the nightstand, silencing it before the sound could reach Marcus. He lay frozen, listening for any whisper of life from his son’s room down the hall, his chest tight with a fear he could not name. Silence. The kind that pressed in rather than released. Marcus needed the rest; lately, a quiet had settled over the boy that felt less like peace and more like retreat—a hollow echo of the child he used to be.

He swung his legs over the side of the bed, the worn floorboards cool against his bare feet. Dawn bled through the curtains, staining the room in shades of gray and muted blue. Sharon lay beside him, her back turned, her breathing even. The alarm had not touched her sleep; she never stirred for anything less than her own internal clock.

Cold water on his face did nothing to loosen the knot in his chest. In the mirror, he traced the lines that had deepened across his forehead over the past year—a map of worries he could not set down. He dressed in the half-dark, his thoughts already turning toward the work that waited: the clean architecture of code, the quiet satisfactions of a problem solved. It was not just the logic, though that was part of it. There was something else—a conversation conducted in the space between intention and machine, a collaboration that asked nothing he could not give. Most people would not understand. He had stopped trying to explain it.

By six-thirty he stood in the kitchen, pouring cereal into a bowl for Marcus while the coffee maker hissed and gurgled. The house held its breath around him. He glanced at the clock: soon Marcus would appear. Sharon would not. She rarely surfaced before eight, and even then her attention belonged to the glow of her phone or a single cup of tea, cradled in silence.

Ed sipped his coffee, leaning against the counter, his gaze drifting toward the living room. Toys lay scattered across the floor—constellations of abandoned play. Dust turned slowly in the shafts of pale morning light falling through the windows, settling on the spines of books on the shelf: The Little Prince, its cover faded; The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe; a row of tattered Mr. Men books, their grinning faces fixed and vacant. He remembered Marcus curled among these stories, his small fingers tracing the illustrations, his voice bright with questions that chased every corner of possibility. That boy was harder to find now. The shape of him remained, but something essential had gone quiet.

Ed’s chest tightened. Where was Marcus’s laughter, his restless curiosity? Buried somewhere beneath the silence, he feared. He had to find a way back to him—had to reach through whatever was closing around his son before the light inside him went out entirely.

Footsteps whispered from the hallway, light and hesitant. Marcus appeared in the doorway, his hair still tangled from sleep, his pajama top hanging loose over his thin frame. He stood silhouetted against the morning light, unmoving.

“Morning, champ,” Ed said, shaping a smile he did not entirely feel. “You hungry?”

Marcus nodded, a slow, heavy motion, and shuffled to his usual place at the table. Ed set the bowl in front of him and reached out to ruffle his hair, the familiar gesture feeling suddenly fragile. “Big plans today? Anything fun?” He kept his voice light, searching Marcus’s downcast face for something—any flicker of the old enthusiasm.

“Dunno.” Marcus’s eyes stayed fixed on the milk swirling in his bowl.

Ed felt the answer land like a stone in his chest. The quiet was not unfamiliar, but something in the flatness of Marcus’s voice, the emptiness in his gaze, felt different. Wrong. He opened his mouth to press further, but Sharon’s voice drifted from the bedroom, thin with annoyance.

“Ed? You still here?”

“Yeah. Just getting Marcus sorted.”

“Oh. Okay.” A pause, then the soft click of her phone unlocking. Ed let out a breath and turned back to his son.

“You feeling alright, buddy? Something on your mind?”

Marcus shook his head without looking up. Ed watched him for a long moment, then glanced at the clock. The commute was already pressing against the edges of his morning. He picked up his briefcase and bent to press a kiss to the top of Marcus’s head.

“Have a good day, champ. See you tonight.”

“Bye, Daddy.”

The whisper was so faint Ed almost missed it. He hesitated, an old instinct telling him to stay, to push a little harder, coax a little more. But something held him back—a premonition that the silence would only harden if he pressed against it. He straightened and walked out into the crisp morning air, letting the cold brace him.

He told himself it was nothing. Just tired. Kids go through phases. But the words tasted hollow. The image of Marcus, swallowed by the quiet at the breakfast table, burned itself into his mind. The abandoned toys, the dust on the bookshelves—they seemed to whisper something he could not quite hear. Something was wrong. He felt it in his bones as he merged into the flow of traffic, a fear sharper than the morning commute, honed to a single point: he did not recognize his son anymore, and he did not know how to find him.

The familiar crunch of gravel under the tires announced his return home. Six in the evening, the light already beginning to soften. He killed the engine and sat for a moment, letting the silence settle around him before stepping inside.

The house was quiet, but not empty. From the living room came the murmur of voices—Sharon on a video call, her laughter bright and easy, carrying through the still air. Ed set his briefcase down and peered around the corner.

Sharon was curled on the couch, her face lit by the screen, talking to her sister. Her laughter cut through the hush of the house, sharp and unguarded. And in the corner of the room, Marcus stood like a sentinel—shoulders slumped, arms hanging loose at his sides, his gaze fixed on his mother. He was not part of the scene. He was watching it, a spectator in his own home.

Ed felt something cold close around his gut. “Hey,” he said, keeping his voice even.

Sharon glanced up, her eyes flicking to him for a moment before returning to the screen. “Oh, hi. Just catching up with Lisa.” The words were casual, but they landed like a door closing.

He forced a smile and turned to Marcus. “Hey, champ. What’s going on?”

The boy did not answer. His eyes were still on Sharon, fixed and empty, as if he were looking at something very far away.

“Marcus?” Ed repeated, a note of worry creeping into his voice.

Sharon waved a hand without looking at him. “He’s fine. Just zoning out.”

Ed bit back a response. He crossed the room and knelt beside Marcus, placing a hand on his shoulder. “You alright, buddy?”

Marcus blinked, as if surfacing from a deep place. His eyes found Ed’s, and for a moment they were clear, present. “Yeah.”

“What were you doing? Watching Mom talk?”

A shrug. Then the word, spoken without inflection, that scraped against Ed’s heart like stone against flint: “Nothing.”

Ed stared at him, searching for a crack, a flicker of the boy he remembered. He found only a stillness that seemed to go down a very long way. He forced himself to stand, swallowing the questions piling up in his throat.

“Alright. Let’s get something to eat.”

Marcus followed him to the kitchen like a sleepwalker, taking his seat at the table while Ed reheated leftovers. The silence between them was thick, charged. Ed cast glances at his son, each one returned with the same vacant gaze.

“So,” Ed said, trying for lightness, “how was your day?”

Marcus looked up, and something passed across his face—a flicker of the boy he used to be, lost and searching. Then the word came again, soft and final: “Nothing.”

Ed’s heart sank. He set a plate in front of Marcus and sat across from him, the space between them suddenly vast. This was not ordinary childhood reticence. It was something else, something that had taken root in the quiet and was spreading.

He would not ignore it. He could not. As he watched his son eat in mechanical silence, a vow formed in the hollow of his chest: he would find his way back to Marcus, would piece together whatever was breaking apart. He owed him that much. Because “nothing” was not an ending. It was a beginning—the beginning of a fight to understand, and to reclaim, the heart of his son.

Days passed in a rhythm Ed had grown to know too well: work, the draining commute, dinner, sleep. But now he watched. He watched Marcus at breakfast, noticing how his son’s eyes slid past his own, how his answers came clipped and distant. He watched Sharon, absorbed in the curated world of her social feed, where their lives appeared seamless and bright. Her posts were a gallery of staged warmth—their home, Marcus, captioned with the language of devoted motherhood. Ed had never questioned it before. Now doubt crept in, thin as a blade.

The house itself seemed to reflect the erosion. Dishes gathered in the sink like ghosts of meals past. Toys lay where they had been dropped, frozen mid-story. Dust settled on the bookshelves, the spines of The Little Prince and The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe standing in silent witness. Ed tried to help, but the hours drained him, leaving little energy for anything beyond the minimum. Sharon had always carried the domestic weight. Now he was no longer certain she was carrying it at all.

One evening, he came home to a scene that stopped him cold. Sharon was on the couch again, phone in hand, laughter spilling from her as she spoke to someone on the screen. And Marcus stood in the corner, motionless, his face a blank mask. He was not playing, not reading, not drawing. He was simply standing, watching a woman who did not watch him back.

Ed set his briefcase down and entered the room, forcing a smile that felt wrong on his face. “Hey.”

Sharon glanced up briefly. “Oh, hi. Just catching up.”

He nodded and turned to Marcus. “Hey, champ. What’s up?”

Silence. Marcus did not seem to hear him.

“Marcus?” His voice tightened.

Sharon waved a hand without looking. “He’s fine. Just zoning out.”

Ed felt the urge to snap at her, sharp and sudden, and bit it back. He knelt beside Marcus and touched his shoulder. “You okay?”

Marcus blinked, surfaced, looked at him with an expression Ed could not read. “Yeah.”

“What were you doing before?”

A shrug. The smallest gesture, and it hit Ed harder than anything else could have. He searched Marcus’s face for some sign of the child beneath the stillness and found only a cold, quiet space.

“Alright,” he murmured. “Let’s get dinner.” Marcus followed him, his gaze drifting back toward Sharon as if tethered to something Ed could not see.

The silence at the table was a presence, dense and suffocating. Ed tried again. “So what did you do today, champ?”

Marcus met his eyes, and for a moment Ed saw not a child’s blankness but a reflection of his own fear. “Nothing.”

Ed’s heart plummeted. The word was no longer just an answer. It was a wall, rising higher each day. He made a silent vow, sharp and unyielding: he would not let “nothing” win.

The hollowness gnawed at Ed with the persistence of something that had made a home inside him. Marcus’s vacant stare was no longer fleeting; it was the new shape of his face. The endless questions, the sudden laughter, the restless energy that once filled every corner of their home—gone. His son moved through the days in a muted drift, a shadow of the boy who had once been all light and motion.

Ed tried everything he could think of. New books appeared on the shelves—The Little Prince again, and others he hoped might catch Marcus’s attention. Board games, puzzles, building blocks. He set them out like offerings, waiting for a spark that did not come. Marcus accepted each one with the same vacant nod, the same weary “Okay,” before retreating to his room, leaving Ed standing in the widening space between them.

One evening, Ed pulled out an old copy of Mr. Tickle from the back of a shelf, its cover soft with handling. He sat beside Marcus on the couch and held it up like a talisman. “Remember these? You used to love them.”

Marcus’s eyes moved to the book, unreadable. “Yeah.”

“Want me to read it to you?”

Something flickered in Marcus’s face—hesitation, and beneath it, a thread of something that might have been longing. He nodded. Ed opened the book and began to read, giving each character a voice: nasal and thin for Mr. Tickle, low and rumbling for Mr. Strong. He coaxed Marcus into joining him, and for a moment, a miracle: the corner of Marcus’s mouth lifted, a smile tentative and fragile, but real. Ed’s heart surged.

“That was fun, wasn’t it?” He was almost afraid to speak, as if the sound might shatter what had just formed.

Marcus nodded again, but this time the light held. “Yeah. Can we do another one tomorrow?”

“Anytime you want,” Ed said, and meant it.

The next evening, Ed walked into a room transformed. The walls were covered in bright sheets of paper, each one filled with meticulously drawn Mr. Men characters—Mr. Happy, Mr. Tickle, Mr. Bump, rendered in bold colors. Sharon stood at the center of it all, marker in hand, adding a final flourish to an elaborate Mr. Happy. The scene was cheerful, bustling, entirely wrong.

“What’s all this?” Ed asked, his voice carefully neutral.

Sharon turned, her smile bright and fixed. “Marcus and I had so much fun with the books yesterday. I thought it would be lovely to draw them together.”

Ed looked at Marcus, who sat at the table in front of a blank sheet of paper, his face empty, his hands still. “Did you have fun doing this?” Ed asked, the words tasting of ash.

A nod, small and mechanical. Ed knelt beside his son. “You okay, champ?”

Marcus shrugged, and then, in a voice barely above a whisper, he said, “I don’t like drawing.”

The words went through Ed like a blade. He understood in that instant: this was not for Marcus. It was for Sharon—a performance of motherhood, a set piece. Her son was the prop, not the participant. Something turned cold in Ed’s chest.

“That’s alright,” he said, keeping his voice steady. “You don’t have to draw. We can just read the books, okay?” He held Marcus’s gaze, trying to telegraph something the boy could hold onto.

Marcus nodded again, the brief light from the night before already dimmed, replaced by a tired acceptance. Ed rose and turned to Sharon, his voice quiet and deliberate. “Maybe we should let Marcus decide what he wants to do.”

Sharon’s smile faltered, a crack in the facade, then smoothed over. “Of course. I just thought it would be fun.”

“Yeah,” Ed said. “Fun.” The word hung in the air between them, stripped of warmth. He looked at Marcus, small and quiet at the table, and understood that this was only the beginning. The fight for his son’s voice, for his light, had just begun.

The silence in the house pressed against Ed like a weight. He lay in bed, each tick of the grandfather clock in the hall hammering out a rhythm of unease. Marcus’s vacant gaze from dinner, Sharon’s forced cheerfulness—it all replayed behind his closed eyes, a portrait of a family coming apart at the seams, one that he could no longer pretend was whole.

He could not name what was wrong, not precisely. But he felt the ground shifting beneath them, a slow tremor that threatened to bring everything down. Accusing Sharon of neglect was unthinkable without proof, but watching Marcus fade felt like its own kind of complicity.

He turned onto his side, the streetlight casting a pale stripe across Sharon’s sleeping face. She breathed deeply, untouched by the turmoil coiling inside him. How could she sleep so soundly while their son was disappearing?

Marcus’s voice echoed in the hollow of his mind: I don’t like drawing. The whisper had been a crack in the wall they had all been pretending was not there. Sharon had taken the Mr. Men books—that fragile moment of connection—and turned it into another project, another performance. Ed remembered Marcus shrinking back, his fingers loosening their grip on the markers. Sharon had not noticed. She never seemed to.

Frustration coiled in Ed’s chest, tight and unyielding. Pretending would not fix this. Hoping it would pass was a lie he could no longer afford to tell himself. Marcus needed him. Needed something Ed was not sure he knew how to give, but he had to try. Maybe confronting Sharon, shattering the fragile peace they had built, was the only way forward.

He closed his eyes, but his mind would not settle. A cold certainty crystallized in the dark: nothing would change unless he acted. And time, he felt with a clarity that cut through the sleepless haze, was running out.

Mornings bled into one another, a string of identical scenes. Marcus at the kitchen table, cereal untouched, his silence as thick as the fog outside the window. Evenings found him retreating to his room after dinner, a ghost moving through a house that had once been full of his presence. In between, Ed performed the motions of normalcy: working, commuting, watching. Watching them both, alert for any sign of change.

One night, desperate, he tried something new. He brought home a dog-eared copy of The Little Prince, hoping its gentle wisdom might reach where his own words could not. He sat beside Marcus on the couch and began to read aloud, giving the story the full weight of his voice. Marcus listened, his eyes on the pages, but his face remained closed, unreachable.

When Ed reached the passage where the fox speaks to the Prince, he paused. “Marcus, the fox says you become responsible forever for what you’ve tamed. What do you think that means?”

A flicker of something crossed Marcus’s face—uncertainty, or the beginning of a thought. “I guess so,” he said, barely above a whisper.

“But what does it mean to you?” Ed pressed, hungry for any thread of connection.

Marcus shrugged, a small, defeated motion. “I don’t know.”

Disappointment pierced Ed, cold and sharp. He forced a nod. “It’s a big thought. We can talk more later, if you want.”

Silence. Marcus traced the edge of a page with his fingertip, as if afraid of the weight it carried. Ed closed the book gently. “Another story? Or maybe a game?”

Marcus shook his head. “Tired.”

Ed’s heart sank. “Alright. Get ready for bed. I’ll tuck you in.”

He watched his son shuffle down the hall, each step a small retreat, and felt the space between them widening into something he did not know how to cross. Marcus was pulling away, deeper into himself, and Ed stood at the edge of that distance, helpless.

He sat alone in the quiet, the abandoned book lying on the coffee table. The fox’s words echoed in his mind, no longer comforting but accusatory: You become responsible forever for what you’ve tamed. Marcus was his responsibility, and yet Ed felt complicit in the slow unraveling, powerless to stop it. He walked to Marcus’s door and peered through the crack. His son lay under the covers, eyes squeezed shut—sleep or escape, Ed could not tell. He whispered a goodnight into the stillness and closed the door. Alone again, the questions circled: What now? How did he reach a boy who was slipping away into a silence he could not breach?

The fluorescent light of the hallway buzzed overhead as Ed came in one evening. Sharon sat on the couch, phone in one hand, a cup of tea cooling untouched on the table beside her. She wore a smile that did not reach her eyes. On the floor, Marcus sat with a coloring book open before him, a single crayon lying discarded beside it. He was not coloring. He was simply sitting, a monument to enforced activity.

“Hey, champ,” Ed said, crouching beside him. “Working on something cool?”

Marcus’s eyes lifted, vacant and brittle. “Nothing.”

Ed picked up the book. A few pages had been colored in neatly—Sharon’s work, he guessed—while most lay untouched. The scene echoed their life: a bright performance laid over a vast emptiness. “Did you start this today?”

Marcus nodded. “Mom said I should.”

Ed glanced at Sharon, whose attention remained fixed on her screen. “Do you even like coloring anymore?” he asked gently, searching Marcus’s face for a trace of the enthusiasm that had once been effortless.

Another shrug. “It’s okay.”

The emptiness in his son’s voice settled into Ed’s chest like a stone. Sharon finally looked up, her expression one of mild surprise, as if she had just noticed they were there.

“Ed, you’re overthinking this. He’s fine. Kids have phases.” Her tone was light, dismissive, each word a small dismissal.

“This isn’t a phase, Sharon,” Ed said, his voice tighter than he intended. But he could feel the argument slipping away from him already. She would not see it. Would not let herself. “Maybe we should let him choose what he wants to do.”

He steered Marcus away from the coloring book and toward a puzzle on the shelf, hoping for a different kind of engagement. But Marcus moved through the motions mechanically, placing pieces without interest, his mind elsewhere. Ed watched Sharon watching them, her presence performative even now, and felt the truth settle over him like a cold weight: he was losing Marcus—not in a single dramatic moment, but slowly, quietly, piece by piece. Each unanswered question, each hollow gaze, wore away at the hope he had been clinging to. He did not just want to see his son again. He needed to reach him, before the silence swallowed him entirely.

The early morning was the worst time. The quiet pressed against Ed from all sides, thick and suffocating. Dawn bled into the kitchen, painting the counters in shades of bruised blue. He stood at the window, coffee in hand, staring out at a world that seemed to be moving forward without him.

He needed to make a decision. A real one. The fear that had been gnawing at him had sharpened into something more precise: time was slipping through his fingers, and he could not afford to wait any longer. Sharon’s world of curated surfaces felt insurmountably distant now, leaving Ed alone in the desolate country of his own worry.

He looked down at his coffee, the surface clouded with a film of thoughts he could not hold onto. He needed to reach Marcus, but the silence between them had grown too wide to bridge with ordinary words. Confronting Sharon was the only way forward, but how could he name something as intangible as the hollowing of a child’s gaze, the slow retreat of his laughter, the pleas for attention that went unnoticed?

No more waiting. No more hoping that things would resolve on their own. Ed set his coffee down and straightened. He needed to become something more than a provider—a steady presence, a lighthouse in the fog that had settled over their home. Sharon needed to see, truly see, the boy she was losing. And if she would not see on her own, he would force the confrontation.

He climbed the stairs to Marcus’s room. His son lay curled under the covers, small against the expanse of the bed, his face slack with sleep. Ed knelt beside him and spoke quietly, not wanting to wake him but needing to say the words aloud.

“Hey, champ. I’m going to figure this out. I promise.”

Marcus stirred slightly but did not wake. Ed stayed there for a long moment, watching the slow rise and fall of his son’s breathing, and felt something harden inside him—not with anger, but with purpose.

He walked downstairs with a renewed sense of clarity. The conversation he needed to have with Sharon would not be easy. It might tear apart whatever remained of their fragile peace. But Marcus mattered more than that peace. He mattered more than anything.

The pale pre-dawn light filtered through the blinds, casting thin stripes across Ed’s face. Weeks had passed, each one grinding away at the edges of his hope. Sharon remained distant, absorbed in her routines. Marcus continued to fade, like a photograph left too long in the sun. Ed felt the familiar tightness in his chest and recognized it now not as fear, but as resolve. He would not let his son drown in this silence.

He moved through the kitchen, each creak of the floorboards loud in the stillness. Passing Marcus’s room, he stopped. The door was closed, smooth and unremarkable, but it felt like a sealed vault. He pressed his hand flat against the wood and spoke, his voice rough with the weight of unshed tears.

“Marcus. I promise. I’ll find a way. We’ll get through this together.”

The words felt thin, almost absurd in their inadequacy. But they were a start. A vow. Ed straightened, tightened his tie, and walked out into the dawn air. It was cold and sharp, and it cleared his mind. He did not know the exact path ahead, did not have a plan beyond the certainty that he would not stop. As long as Marcus needed him, Ed would keep moving forward. Until the light returned to his son’s eyes, he would not rest.

The weeks blurred together, each one leaving Ed heavier. Marcus’s silence had become a presence in the house, a thing that occupied space. The word “nothing” echoed through their days—Marcus’s answer to every question, the door he closed with each utterance.

Ed tried everything. Board games sat untouched on the coffee table. Books lay open, their pages turned only by the draft from an open window. He asked Marcus questions about school, about friends, about anything, and met the same flat refusals: “It’s okay,” “Don’t know,” or simply silence.

Then one evening, Ed noticed Marcus looking at the gaming console, his gaze lingering in a way it had not done in weeks. It was a small thing, barely a flicker of interest, but Ed seized it.

“Hey, Marcus, want to try a game? There’s one called It Takes Two. You have to work together to solve the puzzles. Could be fun.”

Marcus looked up, a thread of something like curiosity in his eyes. “What game?”

“Co-op puzzle game. Made by the same person who did A Way Out. You remember—the one where the guy said, you know, screw the Oscars.”

A shrug, but it was not entirely without interest. “It’s okay.”

Ed took that as a yes. He set up the console and handed Marcus a controller. For the first few minutes, Marcus fumbled with the buttons, his movements uncertain. But as they moved through the game’s world together, something shifted. Ed watched as concentration settled over his son’s face, his fingers finding the rhythm of the controls. When they solved a tricky puzzle, Marcus let out a small, surprised laugh—a sound so unexpected that Ed felt his heart clench.

“Cool,” Marcus breathed, the ghost of a smile on his lips.

“Told you,” Ed said, his voice lighter than it had been in months. “Want to keep going?”

Marcus nodded, his eyes bright with something Ed had almost forgotten. They played for hours, the evening dissolving into shared concentration and occasional laughter. Ed wove questions into the pauses between levels, careful not to push too hard. Marcus answered some—briefly, but he answered. The wall was still there, but for a few hours, Ed had glimpsed the boy behind it.

He clung to that glimpse, fragile as it was, and vowed to protect it.

The silence returned the next evening, thicker than before. The console sat dormant, its light dark. Marcus was at the kitchen table, a plate of cold pasta in front of him, untouched. He stared at the food without seeing it.

“Hey, champ,” Ed said, setting down his briefcase. “How was your day?”

“It’s okay.” Marcus did not look up.

Ed sat across from him. “Anything fun happen?”

“Not really.”

“Maybe we could play that game again tonight? Finish it together?”

A shrug. “It’s okay.”

Ed felt the hope he had been carrying begin to crack. He reached across the table and rested his hand on Marcus’s arm, light and tentative. “Marcus, you know you can talk to me, right? About anything. Sadness, anger, worries—I’m here.”

Marcus nodded, a tiny motion, barely a response. “It’s okay.”

Ed withdrew his hand and sat back, defeated. The brief connection from the night before felt like a dream now, as insubstantial as smoke. He watched his son push the pasta around his plate and felt the weight of the house pressing down on him—the silence, the distance, the slow erosion of everything they had once been.

The gnawing in Ed’s chest had settled into a dull, persistent ache. Weeks passed, marked by failed attempts to draw Marcus out. Board games gathered dust. A half-built model sat abandoned on the shelf. Even the telescope Ed had bought for stargazing stood untouched, pointed at a sky Marcus no longer seemed interested in exploring.

One evening, Ed brought out a notebook and pen. “Let’s make up a story together,” he said. “You tell it, and I’ll write it down.”

Marcus eyed the notebook with suspicion. “What kind of story?”

“Any kind you want. Superheroes. Spaceships. A talking dog. Whatever you can imagine.”

A long pause. Then Marcus shook his head. “It’s okay.”

“Come on, champ. Remember those dragons you used to tell me about? The ones that lived in the clouds?”

“I don’t know.”

Ed leaned forward, his voice dropping. “Look at me, son. Something is weighing on you. You don’t have to tell me what it is. But I want you to know I’m here. We’ll get through whatever this is, together.”

For a moment, their eyes held. Ed saw something stir in Marcus’s gaze—a flicker of the boy he used to be, the one who had once trusted him without reservation. Then it was gone, and Marcus looked away.

“It’s okay.”

Ed sank back into the couch, the quiet of the house settling around him like a shroud. Sharon sat in the armchair, her phone glowing, her attention elsewhere. He looked at her, and the words came out before he could stop them.

“Sharon. Don’t you see this? He’s hurting. And you’re just sitting there.”

She looked up, annoyance flickering across her face. “Ed, stop catastrophizing. He’s fine. Kids go through phases.”

“Phases? He’s withdrawn into himself. He barely speaks. Does that not concern you at all?”

Sharon sighed, setting her phone down. “He’ll snap out of it. You need to give him space instead of smothering him.”

Ed wanted to argue, to make her feel the fear that had taken root in his chest. But the words would not come. He turned back to Marcus, who sat motionless, a statue in the dim light, and felt the hopelessness press down on him like a physical weight.

“Marcus,” he whispered, resting his hand on the boy’s shoulder. “I’m right here. Whenever you’re ready.”

Marcus did not respond. Ed held his position, refusing to move, refusing to abandon the small, still body beside him to the dark that seemed to be closing in.

The refrigerator hummed in the quiet of the kitchen, the only sound in a house that had grown accustomed to silence. Ed sat hunched over the table, a mug of coffee growing cold in his hands. The steam rose and dissipated, carrying the last remnants of the day’s warmth with it.

He had opened his laptop earlier, typing search terms into the glowing rectangle: child losing interest in activities. The results filled the screen with clinical language and checklists, fragmented narratives of children who had slipped into silence. Another search: signs of emotional neglect. The words hit him like a blow to the chest.

The articles described Marcus with unsettling precision: the withdrawal, the stilled questions, the slow retreat into a private world. A truth began to form in Ed’s mind, cold and undeniable: children who are not heard eventually stop speaking. Had Marcus been trying to tell them something, in his quiet way, and they had failed to listen? Had Ed, blinded by work and exhaustion, let his son drift into invisibility right in front of him?

He looked at his reflection in the dark window. The boy’s haunted gaze seemed to stare back at him, a silent accusation. Doubt coiled in his stomach, sharp and insistent. He had trusted Sharon to be present, to fill the gaps his long hours left open. But she had not been present. She had been there, in the house, but somewhere else entirely.

Ed rose abruptly, the chair scraping against the floor. He did not know what to do next. Confront Sharon. Demand change. The options felt equally impossible.
