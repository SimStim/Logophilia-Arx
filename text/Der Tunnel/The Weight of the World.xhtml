There is a specific kind of vertigo that accompanies the contemplation of the monumental. It is not the dizzying height of a mountain peak, but rather the crushing psychological weight of something far too large to be contained by a single human lifespan. We feel it when we look at the blueprints of a particle accelerator, or the sprawling, neon-lit grids of a cyberpunk megacity, or the terrifyingly vast rings of a Dyson sphere. It is the realization that we are witnessing, participating in, a process that has outgrown the individual.

In the history of speculative fiction, we often celebrate the “Eureka!” moment: the single, brilliant invention that changes the course of history. We worship at the altar of the gadget, the ray gun, and the time machine. But there is another, more somber mode of science fiction. It is a fiction not of the object, but of the system. It is a fiction that asks not “How does this work?” but “What does it cost us to build it?”

This is the realm of Bernhard Kellermann’s Der Tunnel.

I remember the first time I encountered this text, its spine brittle with the dust of almost a century. I expected the whimsical adventure of a Jules Verne: a subterranean voyage or an undersea odyssey. Instead, I found something far more unsettling. I found a novel that felt less like a dream of the future and more like a post-mortem of a civilization. Reading Der Tunnel is not an act of escapism; it is an encounter with the terrifying machinery of modernity.

Published in 1913, on the precipice of a world-shattering war, Der Tunnel arrived as a crystallization of the European zeitgeist. The “Radium Age” was in full bloom, a period defined by a feverish, almost manic optimism regarding technological progress, shadowed by an equal and opposite dread of industrial catastrophe. The era believed that science could conquer nature, yet it was simultaneously beginning to realize that science had also created new, uncontrollable scales of destruction.

Kellermann did not invent the idea of the transatlantic tunnel; engineers and speculators had been dreaming of such feats for decades. But where his predecessors focused on the technical “how,” Kellermann pivoted to the systemic “how much.” He moved the needle of science fiction from invention to system.

In the hands of Verne or Wells, a great machine is often a solitary protagonist. In Der Tunnel, the protagonist is the Megaproject itself: a sprawling, breathing entity composed of global capital, international labor politics, media manipulation, and engineering failure cascades. Kellermann introduces us to “megaproject realism.” He shows us that a tunnel is not merely a feat of boring machines and concrete; it is a web of financing crises, a catalyst for strikes, a magnet for public opinion, and a graveyard for the men who toil within it.

This shift is what places Kellermann in a lineage distinct from the more famous “hard” science fiction writers. When we think of megastructures today, our minds often drift to Arthur C. Clarke. We think of the Space Elevator, a triumph of physics-constrained futurism, where the tension arises from the limits of gravity and materials science. But Kellermann’s vision is different. His tension is capital-constrained. He presents an industrial epic where the primary obstacles are not the laws of physics, but the volatility of markets, the fragility of political alliances, and the sheer, unmanageable scale of human logistics.

To read Der Tunnel is to witness the birth of “socialized” megaproject fiction. Kellermann explores a hauntingly modern question: What kind of civilization destroys itself in the very act of trying to transcend its limits? He anticipates the themes that would later define the Weimar-era dystopias and the industrial nightmares of Fritz Lang. He understands that as our technology grows in scale, it becomes increasingly decoupled from human agency; that every attempt to transcend a boundary might produce a system that no longer recognizes the boundaries set by its creator.

There is a profound, almost prophetic melancholy running through the prose. Kellermann touches upon an idea that feels startlingly contemporary: the notion that a technological achievement becomes obsolete the very moment it is completed. The infrastructure, once realized, becomes a static monument to a past era of ambition, a relic of a struggle that has already moved on to the next catastrophe.

While Der Tunnel may not have become a “vocation myth” like the works of Kurd Laßwitz, inspiring generations of engineers to take up the slide rule, its influence is far more atmospheric and cultural. It did not teach us how to build; it taught us how to fear what we build. It seeped into the DNA of European science fiction, providing the structural blueprint for the industrial dread that would haunt the 20th century.

Ultimately, Kellermann’s achievement was to turn engineering fantasy into a political economy drama. He took the dream of the tunnel and filled it with the sweat of the laborer and the greed of the financier. He showed us that the most dangerous part of any great structure is not the pressure of the earth above it, but the weight of the human systems required to sustain it.

As we stand today in an age of even greater scales—of climate engineering, of global digital infrastructures, of orbital colonies—Der Tunnel remains an essential, chilling guide. It reminds us: when we reach for the impossible, we are not just moving earth and stone; we are rearranging the very foundations of our social contract.
