 Non-fiction Trivia
Inventions and Discoveries

The first-ever patent was granted in Venice on March 19, 1474, for a method of making better water clocks. The idea was to encourage innovation by protecting inventors’ rights.

In 1945, while researching radar at Raytheon, engineer Percy Spencer felt a strange sensation near a magnetron and discovered that a chocolate bar in his pocket was melting. Curious, he placed popcorn kernels near the magnetron, leading to popcorn popping across the lab. He then experimented with an egg, which exploded and splattered a coworker. This confirmed that microwaves could cook food rapidly. Spencer and Raytheon engineers subsequently developed the first microwave oven.

On September 3, 1928, after returning from holiday, Alexander Fleming examined petri dishes with Staphylococcus colonies. He observed one dish with a mold blob—later identified as Penicillium notatum—where bacteria were absent. This clear zone around the mold indicated it was producing a substance that inhibited bacterial growth.

Thomas Edison is often credited with inventing the light bulb, but he actually improved on an earlier design by Hiram Maxim’s, who worked on lamps as chief engineer at the United States Electric Lighting Company since June 1878. Maxim’s patent was only granted in July 1880, while Edison’s, filed in 1879, was approved in January 1880. Maxim was so upset by this that he went on to invent the rapid-fire machine gun. While the link between Edison’s patent and Maxim’s invention is anecdotal, it is true that Maxim also invented the machine gun.

J.C.R. Licklider first proposed the concept of the Internet in 1962. On October 29, 1969, at 10:30 p.m. PST, the first successful message was transmitted over ARPANET, the Internet’s precursor. Licklider’s ideas anticipated most elements of today’s Internet, including cloud computing.

The Analytical Engine, designed by English mathematician Charles Babbage, was a proposed general-purpose mechanical computer first described in 1837. Intended as a successor to Babbage’s simpler Difference Engine, it was never completed but laid the foundation for modern computing.

The Space Shuttle, developed by NASA, was the first reusable spacecraft, with its first flight occurring in 1981. It was designed to make space travel more economical and accessible.

Post-it Notes were created by accident. Dr. Spencer Silver, a scientist at 3M, was trying to create a strong adhesive but instead developed a repositionable glue, which led to the creation of Post-it Notes.

Alexander Graham Bell is credited with inventing the telephone, but his rival Elisha Gray filed a similar patent on the same day. The two engaged in a legal battle over the invention.

In 1941, on a hunting trip, George de Mestral noticed burs clinging to his pants and his dog’s fur. Inspired, he envisioned a synthetic fabric mimicking this mechanism, offering a new fastening method between buttons, zippers, and sewing. By combining hooks, like those in burs, with fabric loops, he created a fastener where the hooks catch the loops. De Mestral patented his invention in 1955 and secured a $150,000 loan to develop it further. He founded a company to produce Velcro, named from “velvet” and “crochet” (French for “hook”).

Edwin Herbert Land invented the instant Polaroid camera in 1947, allowing people to take and develop photographs in under a minute.

Mechanical engineer Richard James invented the Slinky by accident in 1943 while working on springs to stabilize ship equipment at sea. He accidentally knocked some samples off a shelf and was amazed to see them gracefully “walk” down instead of just falling.

The voltaic pile, invented by Italian chemist Alessandro Volta in 1799, was the first electrical battery capable of continuously providing an electric current to a circuit.

The discovery of antibiotics like penicillin led to antibiotic resistance, a phenomenon where bacteria evolve to resist drugs. This discovery has become a significant challenge in modern medicine.

Frank Whittle, an English engineer, and Hans von Ohain, a German engineer, independently developed the jet engine in the 1930s. This invention revolutionized air travel.

Dr. Raymond Damadian invented the first MRI (Magnetic Resonance Imaging) machine in 1977, revolutionizing medical imaging by allowing detailed views of internal body structures.

Vint Cerf and Robert Kahn developed the Internet Protocol Suite (TCP/IP) in the 1970s, which became the foundation for modern networking and the Internet.

Dean Kamen invented the Segway Personal Transporter in 2001. The self-balancing scooter was designed to revolutionize personal transportation, although it became more of a niche product.

In 1961, Black & Decker introduced the cordless drill under the “DeWalt” brand, revolutionizing the construction and DIY industries by offering greater convenience and mobility compared to traditional corded drills.

Röntgen accidentally discovered X-rays while experimenting with fluorescence in vacuum tubes. Using a shielded Crookes cathode tube, he noticed that rays emitted from the tube passed through the shielding and created a shadow-like image on nearby light-sensitive paper.

The invention of the printing press is traditionally attributed to Johannes Gutenberg, who revolutionized communication by enabling mass production of books and spreading knowledge across Europe. However, recent findings suggest that Procopius Waldvogel (also known as Prokop Waldvogel or Procopius Waldfogel), a German silversmith and medieval printer from Avignon, may have invented printing before Gutenberg. Fleeing from Prague during the Hussite troubles, Waldvogel settled in Lucerne, Switzerland, and later arrived in Avignon in 1444. In 2015, two Hebrew-letter quires from the 16th century were found, and analysis indicated they could have been printed in Avignon around 1444, possibly by Waldvogel and Davin de Caderousse. Waldvogel’s name appears in several contracts from that period, most notably one in which he agrees to provide Davin de Caderousse with movable metal type for Hebrew letters. Since 2019, three independent studies have confirmed this conclusion through further analysis of paper, watermark, and typography. The Gutenberg Bible, on the other hand, is an edition of the Latin Vulgate printed later in the 1450s.

Developed in the late 20th century, the Internet revolutionized global communication and access to information. Tim Berners-Lee’s creation of the World Wide Web in 1989 enabled the sharing of documents and resources over interconnected computer networks.

James Watt’s improvements to the steam engine in the 18th century powered the Industrial Revolution, driving machinery, transportation, and manufacturing. The modified steam engines not only reduced waste but also cut fuel costs. Watt’s steam engine design became foundational for modern engines and power generation.

Edward Jenner’s development of the smallpox vaccine in 1796 initiated the practice of vaccination, preventing infectious diseases through immunization. Jenner used cowpox virus to induce immunity against smallpox, ultimately eradicating the disease globally.

Samuel Morse’s invention of the telegraph and Morse code in the 1830s and 1840s revolutionized long-distance communication. Morse code, a system of dots and dashes representing letters and numbers, enabled rapid transmission of messages over telegraph wires.

Philo Farnsworth’s invention of the electronic television in the 1920s revolutionized entertainment and information dissemination. Farnsworth developed the image dissector principle in the summer of 1921, just before his 15th birthday, and demonstrated the first working version on September 7, 1927, shortly after turning 21. A farm boy, he was inspired by the back-and-forth motion of plowing a field to scan an image as a series of lines. Farnsworth’s working model successfully transmitted moving images, laying the foundation for modern broadcasting.

James Watson and Francis Crick’s discovery of the double helix structure of DNA in 1953 elucidated the genetic blueprint of life. Their model revealed how DNA molecules store and transmit genetic information, advancing genetics and molecular biology.

The Benz Patent-Motorwagen, built in 1885 by German inventor Karl Benz, is widely considered the first practical modern automobile and the first car to enter production. It was patented in January 1886 and publicly unveiled later that year. The motor vehicle enabled personal mobility and paved the way for modern automotive engineering and manufacturing.

Antonie van Leeuwenhoek’s development of the microscope in the 17th century enabled the observation of microorganisms and cells. Leeuwenhoek’s high-quality lenses allowed scientists to explore the microscopic world, laying the foundation for microbiology and medical research.

Louis Daguerre’s invention of the daguerreotype in 1839 introduced photography as a medium for capturing and preserving images. Daguerre’s process involved exposing treated plates to light, producing detailed photographs and pioneering the art and science of photography.

Guglielmo Marconi’s development of wireless telegraphy in the late 19th century enabled radio communication across long distances. Marconi’s radio transmission of Morse code signals demonstrated the potential for wireless communication, leading to the development of radio broadcasting.

Joseph Lister’s introduction of antiseptic surgical practices in 1867 revolutionized medicine by reducing infections during surgeries. Lister used carbolic acid to sterilize surgical instruments and wounds, pioneering antiseptic techniques that improved patient outcomes.

The earliest evidence of the wheel predates the Mesopotamian theories, with a clay model of a bull on four wheels found in the Carpathian Mountains of western Ukraine. Carbon-dated to 3950–3650 B.C.E., it is hundreds of years older than any wheeled vehicles in Mesopotamia. The wheel’s invention revolutionized transportation and mechanical technology, leading to the development of wheeled vehicles that greatly enhanced trade, agriculture, and engineering advancements.

In the late 19th century, the Lumière brothers, Auguste and Louis, revolutionized motion pictures with their Cinématographe. Originally patented by Léon Guillaume Bouly on February 12, 1892, the device could record, develop, and project films. The Lumières improved upon this invention and patented their version on February 13, 1895. Their pioneering short films, including “Arrival of a Train at La Ciotat,” captivated audiences and laid the foundation for the film industry, significantly shaping modern cinema.

Carl von Linde revolutionized food preservation with his development of mechanical refrigeration systems in 1875. Initially, he used methyl alcohol as the refrigerant in his first compression machine. By the following year, he introduced a compression system using ammonia as the refrigerant. Linde’s advancements in refrigeration technology made possible the creation of cold storage facilities and the efficient distribution of perishable goods.

Crawford Williamson Long’s use of ether as an anesthetic during surgery on May 30, 1842, revolutionized pain management and surgical procedures. Long’s discovery paved the way for safer and more effective anesthesia techniques, improving patient comfort and medical outcomes.

On May 16, 1960, Theodore Maiman demonstrated the first working laser at Hughes Research Laboratory in California, using a high-power flash lamp to excite a ruby rod with silver-coated surfaces. Although his initial report was rejected by Physical Review Letters, Maiman’s invention introduced laser technology, transforming scientific, industrial, and medical fields. His ruby laser emitted coherent light pulses, enabling precise cutting, welding, and medical procedures, and revolutionized various applications.

In the 1950s at Bell Labs in Murray Hill, New Jersey, scientists Daryl Chapin, Calvin Fuller, and Gerald Pearson worked with thin strips of silicon to enhance its electrical conductivity. After experimenting with various elements, they connected an ammeter to the silicon and tested it under a lamp. When the light was turned on, they immediately realized their silicon solar cell could generate power, marking a breakthrough in solar energy technology. Solar cells convert sunlight directly into electrical energy, promoting renewable energy adoption and sustainability.

Leo Baekeland’s invention of Bakelite, the first synthetic plastic, in 1907 revolutionized materials science and manufacturing. Bakelite’s heat-resistant and versatile properties paved the way for mass production of plastic products, transforming industries from consumer goods to aerospace.

The invention of public-key cryptography in the 1970s by Whitfield Diffie, Martin Hellman, and Ralph Merkle revolutionized secure communication and digital encryption. Public-key encryption algorithms enable secure data transmission over networks, safeguarding privacy and electronic transactions.
