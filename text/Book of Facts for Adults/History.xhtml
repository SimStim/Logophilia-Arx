 Non-fiction Trivia
History

Napoleon Bonaparte was once attacked by a horde of rabbits. During a hunt, a large group of rabbits were released, and instead of fleeing, they charged at him. This story was published in the 1890s in the French text “Mémoires du général Bon Thiébault,” attributed to Paul Thiébault, a general in Napoleon’s army. Although Thiébault died in 1846, many of his histories and memoirs were published posthumously. In this case, the book indicates that this version was published by his daughter, based on the original manuscript by Fernand Calmettes.

Cleopatra lived closer in time to the Moon landing than to the construction of the Great Pyramid. The Great Pyramid was built around 2560 BCE, while Cleopatra lived around 30 BCE, and the Moon landing was in 1969 CE.

Following the death of Israel’s first president, Chaim Weizmann, Albert Einstein was offered the presidency of Israel. He declined the offer in 1952, stating he lacked the experience to deal with people properly.

In ancient Rome, urine was used as a mouthwash. It contained ammonia, which acted as a cleaning agent. Ancient Romans not only sold pee collected from public urinals, but those who traded in urine had to pay a tax.

The shortest war in history lasted between 38 and 45 minutes (the 38-minute duration is the most often quoted). The Anglo–Zanzibar War was fought between the United Kingdom and the Sultanate of Zanzibar on August 27, 1896.

In medieval times, animals were put on trial for crimes and, if found guilty, could be executed or exiled. These trials were recorded in Europe from the thirteenth to the eighteenth century, with the most documented cases occurring in France. However, such trials also took place in Italy, Portugal, Spain, and other countries.

In the late 18th and early 19th centuries, dentures were commonly made from the teeth of deceased soldiers. Teeth were collected from battlefields after significant battles, such as the Battle of Waterloo.

President John F. Kennedy donated his entire presidential salary to charity. He did the same with his congressional salary.

The ancient city of Rome had a population density similar to modern-day Manhattan. Despite its ancient infrastructure, Rome was densely populated.

In December 1931, while on a lecture tour in New York to recover his 1929 stock market crash losses, Churchill was looking for his friend Bernard Baruch’s apartment. Around 10:30 p.m. on December 13, 1931, Churchill attempted to cross Fifth Avenue and was struck by a car, narrowly escaping death. The accident happened because Churchill forgot that in the United States, traffic on two-way roads keeps to the right, unlike in his native United Kingdom where it keeps to the left.

During the Great Depression, bank robbers like Bonnie and Clyde were seen as heroes. They were romanticized as symbols of resistance against the banks.

The oldest known living tree is 4,856 years old. It’s a bristlecone pine named Methuselah, located in California. According to the Gymnosperm Database, “it seems likely the tree had 4,789 rings (crossdated) in the summer of 1957.” The age of 4,856 years in 2024 is based on this reference point. A bristlecone pine named Prometheus, located in Wheeler Peak, Nevada, was cut down by Donald Rusk Currey in 1964 and was believed to have been more than 4,900 years old.

On July 17, 1917, during the third year of World War I, Britain’s King George V ordered the British royal family to abandon their German titles and surnames. He changed his own family’s surname from the decidedly Germanic Saxe-Coburg-Gotha to Windsor.

In the 18th century, pineapple rental was a status symbol in England. Pineapples were so rare and expensive that they were rented out for display at parties.

The first known contraceptive was crocodile dung, used by the Egyptians in 2000 BCE. It was used as a pessary, inserted into the vagina to block sperm.

Hiroo Onoda, a Japanese second lieutenant in World War II, continued guerrilla warfare on Lubang Island, Philippines, for nearly 29 years after 1945. He engaged in shootouts and evaded capture, initially with one soldier who surrendered in 1950 and two others who were killed in 1954 and 1972. Finally persuaded by his former commanding officer, Major Yoshimi Taniguchi, who flew to Lubang, Onoda surrendered on March 10, 1974. He returned to Japan to a hero’s welcome for his extraordinary endurance and dedication to his wartime duty.

Operation Fortitude, part of Allied deception strategy for the 1944 Normandy landings, aimed to mislead the German High Command on invasion locations. Split into North and South subplans, it involved phantom field armies in Edinburgh and southern England (Fortitude North and South), simulating threats to Norway and Pas de Calais. The operation successfully diverted Axis forces from Normandy and delayed German reinforcements post-D-Day.

The 1961 Goldsboro B-52 crash near Goldsboro, North Carolina, involved a Boeing B-52 carrying two 3.8-megaton nuclear bombs. During the accident, the aircraft broke up mid-air, causing the bombs to drop. Pilot Walter Scott Tulloch ordered the crew to eject at 9,000 feet. Five crew members survived by ejecting safely or bailing out, while three others tragically perished. Declassified information since 2013 revealed that one bomb was dangerously close to detonation, highlighting significant safety concerns at the time.

During the Prohibition Era, the federal government mandated toxic additives in industrial alcohols to deter bootleg liquor production. This policy, enforced through law enforcement raids, tax audits, and surveillance, inadvertently led to deaths from poisoned spirits among Americans.

Cleopatra VII, the last Pharaoh of Egypt, was not ethnically Egyptian; she was of Macedonian Greek descent.

Napoleon Bonaparte was not short, he was actually of average height for his time. Napoleon’s height is estimated around 5 feet 2 inches in pre-metric French measures, approximately 1.67 meters or just under 5 feet 6 inches in imperial measure. Some sources suggest he may have been 1.7 meters or 5 feet 7 inches, measured later on a British island after France adopted the metric system. Napoleon’s stature appeared shorter due to his presence among tall Imperial Guards, chosen for their height, contributing to the perception of his height.

The Emu War, conducted in Australia in late 1932, aimed to control emu populations damaging crops in Western Australia’s Campion district. Royal Australian Artillery soldiers, armed with Lewis guns, were deployed but failed to significantly reduce the emu numbers. The media dubbed the operation the “Emu War” due to its military nature. Despite casualties among the emus, they continued to pose a threat by destroying crops even after the campaign. The emus won the war.

The marathon originates from the tale of Pheidippides, an ancient Greek soldier who reputedly ran from Marathon to Athens to announce victory. Despite popular belief, historian Herodotus did not document this story in his Histories. Scholars suggest it may be a romantic invention, with Lucian being the only classical source containing all elements of the myth.

The Yam, known as Örtöö in Mongolian, was a crucial postal and messenger system expanded by Ögedei Khan and later khans. It featured relay stations offering food, shelter, and horses, vital for Mongol armies traveling swiftly across 200–300 kilometers daily. Ögedei Khan prioritized Yam for rapid communication and intelligence gathering. After the Golden Horde’s breakup, the system persisted in the Russian Tsardom.

Gustave Eiffel constructed a private apartment for himself at the top of the Eiffel Tower’s third level. Despite its modest size, the cozy space featured warm wallpaper, soft chintzes, wooden cabinets, and a grand piano, reflecting a simple and comfortable style favored by scientists. Adjacent laboratory areas housed experimental equipment of the time, creating a unique living and working space nearly 1,000 feet above ground.

The first recorded use of chemical warfare occurred in 256 AD during the Siege of Dura–Europos by the Persians. They dug tunnels under the city walls, and the Romans countered by tunneling to reach them. When Romans breached a Persian tunnel, attackers ignited a sulfur and pitch mixture, releasing sulfur dioxide gas, killing twenty Roman soldiers. A coin dated 256, found among the bodies, helped date the siege. Archaeologists excavated the site in the 1930s, and 2009 tests confirmed sulfur dioxide presence. In 2020, Foxborough, Massachusetts chemistry students analyzed samples, concluding that methane was also likely a by-product of the attack.

On September 19, 1893, Governor Lord Glasgow signed into law a new Electoral Act, making New Zealand the first self-governing country to legally grant women the right to vote in parliamentary elections.

Brain surgery has a longer history than commonly thought. Trepanation, the practice of drilling holes in the skull, dates back over 5000 years in Europe, making it the oldest known surgical procedure from antiquity. Evidence suggests that ancient humans survived these procedures.

Pope Julius II personally commanded the papal forces in the successful Siege of Mirandola. Despite setbacks and heavy losses at the Battle of Ravenna, he eventually compelled Louis XII’s French troops to withdraw beyond the Alps with the aid of Swiss mercenaries from the Holy Roman Empire.

Ancient Roman concrete, known for its durability, continues to stand in numerous structures today. Unlike modern concrete, which is designed to be inert, Roman concrete interacts with its environment. When exposed to seawater, it forms rare minerals like aluminous tobermorite and phillipsite, enhancing its strength over time.

The earliest recorded peace treaty was between the Egyptians and the Hittites. In 1259 BC, 15 years after the Battle of Qadesh, Hittite king Hattusili III and pharaoh Ramesses II drafted a treaty aimed at securing “peace and brotherhood for all time.” Two separate versions were written, one in the Hittite capital Hattusa and the other in Ramesses’s capital Pi-Ramesse.

In ancient Chinese history, the earliest books were written on bamboo and wood. These materials served not only for everyday writing but also for recording literary texts. Thin bamboo strips were commonly bound together with string and rolled up. Instead of engraving, ink and a brush or stylus were used to write on these strips.

Before the late sixteenth century, Basque sailor families started growing potatoes along the Biscay coast in northern Spain. Sir Walter Raleigh introduced potatoes to Ireland around 1589, planting them on 40,000 acres of land near Cork. It took nearly forty years for potatoes to spread across the rest of Europe.

The world’s oldest recorded joke dates back to 1900 BC and reflects the enduring popularity of toilet humor, even in ancient times. It originates from the Sumerians, who lived in present-day southern Iraq, and goes: “Something which has never happened since time immemorial; a young woman did not fart in her husband’s lap.”

Ancient Egyptians wrote love poems, some of which have survived on papyrus scrolls.

The first known seismoscope was invented in China by Zhang Heng of China’s Han dynasty in 132 AD.

Viking women experienced significant social freedom. They could own property, seek divorce if mistreated, and shared responsibility in managing farms and homesteads alongside men. Laws protected them from various forms of unwanted male advances.

The ancient Olympic Games, by the fifth century BC, spanned five full days and included events such as running, jumping, throwing, boxing, wrestling, pankration, and chariot racing.

Sticky rice was incorporated into the mortar used to build the Great Wall of China, enhancing its strength significantly. Research by modern chemists has identified amylopectin, a type of polysaccharide found in rice and other starchy foods, as a key factor contributing to the durability and robustness of sticky rice mortar.

Ancient Romans possessed a multi-tool akin to a Swiss Army knife. This folding utensil included a three-pronged fork, spoon, spatula, pick, spike, and an iron knife (now eroded). Each item could be folded out as needed via a hinge, ensuring ease of use and transport. The spike likely aided in extracting meat from snails, the spatula in scraping sauce from narrow-necked bottles, and some have theorized the pick with a tiny spoon end may have been used for earwax removal.

The Galenic and Hippocratic traditions of ancient Greece held the belief that orgasm, or at least sexual arousal, played a reproductive role in both sexes. In other words, they believed that female orgasm was necessary for conception, not just male ejaculation.
