 Non-fiction Trivia
Animals and Zoology

The fastest animal on Earth is the peregrine falcon. When in a hunting dive, known as a stoop, peregrine falcons can reach speeds of over 242 miles per hour (389 kilometers per hour), making them the fastest animal in terms of flight speed.

When considering land animals, the fastest is the cheetah, which can reach speeds of 68–75 miles per hour (109.4–120.7 kilometers per hour) in short bursts covering distances up to 500 meters (1,640 feet). The cheetah can accelerate from 0 to 96.6 km/h (60.0 mph) in under three seconds.

In the water, the black marlin holds the record, capable of swimming at speeds of up to 82 miles per hour (132 kilometers per hour). This speed was measured by a hooked black marlin stripping line off a fishing reel at 120 feet per second.

The horsefly, specifically the species Hybomitra hinei wrighti, is considered the fastest flying invertebrate, with speeds recorded up to 90 miles per hour (145 kilometers per hour), measured by interpolation of slow-motion cinematography.

Dolphins have unique identifiers: Each dolphin develops its own distinctive whistle, functioning like a name, which they use to communicate with one another. Dolphins mimic the signature whistles of their loved ones, such as their mother or a close male companion, especially when separated. These “names” are exclusively used in friendly contexts and are never employed in aggressive or antagonistic situations.

Cows form close friendships: Research has demonstrated that cows develop strong bonds with specific other cows and experience stress when they are separated from their companions.

Sea otters hold hands while sleeping to stay together and prevent drifting apart. This charming behavior, known as “rafting,” is one of nature’s most endearing sights. Additionally, sea otters use rocks as tools to crack open shells and often keep their favorite rocks tucked under their armpits.

The heart of a blue whale is so large that it is capable of fitting a human head inside its arteries.

Sloths defecate just once a week and descend from trees to do so, making them susceptible to predators. Remarkably, they can store up to a third of their body weight in feces.

Mantis shrimp have the most complex eyes in the animal kingdom. Compared to the three types of photoreceptor cells in human eyes, mantis shrimp eyes have between 12 and 16 types of photoreceptor cells. Additionally, some mantis shrimp species can adjust the sensitivity of their long-wavelength color vision to adapt to their environment. This species-specific phenomenon is known as “spectral tuning.”

Elephants are capable of recognizing themselves in a mirror, placing them in the exclusive group of self-aware animals that includes great apes (such as humans) and bottlenose dolphins.

Male seahorses are the ones that give birth. The females deposit eggs into the males’ brood pouch, where he fertilizes and carries them until they hatch.

The slow loris is the only venomous primate. It has a toxic bite that results from licking a gland on its inner elbow, known as the brachial organ. The secretion from this gland becomes activated when mixed with the loris’s saliva.

Sharks have been around longer than trees. Sharks have existed for about 400 million years, while the first trees appeared around 350 million years ago.

A group of owls is called a “parliament” because owls are often associated with wisdom. In Greek mythology, owls symbolize Athena, the goddess of wisdom and reason.

Hummingbirds are the only birds capable of flying backward for extended periods and can even hover upside down. This remarkable maneuverability is due to their unique wing structure.

The axolotl, a type of salamander, possesses the extraordinary ability to regenerate not just its limbs, but also its ovaries, lung tissue, parts of its brain and spinal cord, heart, and various other organs.

Komodo dragons have powerful venom that works gradually. The dragon patiently tracks its weakening prey until it is too exhausted to stand, and only then does it move in for an easy kill.

The ability to recognize individual faces was once believed to be unique to large-brained mammals. However, recent studies have revealed that some wasps can also distinguish between individual faces. Additionally, honeybees have been shown to recognize human faces, a skill that aids them in identifying specific flowers and navigating intricate environments.

Naked mole-rats are resistant to cancer and can survive without oxygen for up to 18 minutes by resorting to the anaerobic metabolism of fructose rather than glucose.

The pistol shrimp, measuring about 4 cm and weighing 25 grams, can snap its claws at 97 km/h, creating a bubble that generates a shockwave. This snap produces a sound of 218 dB, louder than a gunshot, and a brief flash of light. The internal pressure of the bubble can reach 4800°C, akin to the sun’s surface temperature, though over a tiny area. The shrimp’s snap generates a water jet traveling at 25 m/s and a pressure of 80 kPa, which is effective in stunning or killing small fish more than the heat or light produced.

Unlike other kangaroos, tree kangaroos live in trees and have developed strong forelimbs and curved claws for climbing.

The lyrebird can mimic nearly any sound it hears, including chainsaws, car alarms, and human voices, as part of its mating display. Check it out in a BBC Wildlife video.

Vampire bats are the only mammals that feed entirely on blood, and they have heat sensors on their noses to locate blood vessels near the skin.

Archerfish can accurately shoot jets of water from their mouths to dislodge insects from branches up to five feet (1.5 meters) above the water. This remarkable ability involves precise aiming and the calculation of refraction angles.

Cuttlefish have up to millions of chromatophores, which can rapidly expand or contract to alter skin color and contrast. By manipulating these chromatophores, cuttlefish can change their appearance almost instantly. They use this camouflage for hunting, evading predators, and communication.

Evidence such as DOI:10.54097/4eygvh38 suggests killer whales engage in cultural transmission of learned behaviors, a trait previously thought unique to primates. The study highlights their sophisticated communication, hierarchical social structures influenced by matrilineal lines, and cooperative hunting strategies adapted to prey and environmental conditions.

Leafcutter ants cultivate fungal gardens by using freshly cut leaves as fertilizer. They nurture the fungus by protecting it from pests and molds, and by removing decayed material. In exchange, the fungus serves as a food source for the ants’ larvae.

Hummingbirds have the fastest metabolism of any animal, with their hearts beating 1,200 times per minute while in flight. To sustain this rapid metabolism, they must consume up to twice their body weight in nectar daily.

Pangolins are the only mammals wholly-covered in scales, which they use as armor against predators in the wild.

Emperor penguins can dive to depths of over 550 meters (1,800 feet) and stay underwater for more than 20 minutes while hunting for food.

The mimic octopus can imitate the appearance and movements of more than fifteen different marine species, such as lionfish, flatfish, and sea snakes.

Narwhals are known for their long, spiral tusks, which are actually elongated teeth that can grow up to 10 feet long.

Dung beetles navigate by the Milky Way, using the light from stars to stay oriented while rolling dung balls. Although their compound eyes may not detect individual stars, the beetles use the Milky Way’s glow to maintain a straight path.

Sea cucumbers (Holothuroidea) eject parts of their gut to deter and defend against predators like crabs and fish. These organs are regenerated within a few days by cells inside the sea cucumber.

Giraffes have the same number of neck vertebrae as humans (seven), but each vertebra is much larger, allowing for their long necks.

Kiwi birds have nostrils at the end of their long beaks, allowing them to sniff out insects and other prey hidden underground.

Female spotted hyenas have a clitoris that is 90% as long and the same diameter as a male penis (171 millimeters in length and 22 millimeters in diameter). The formation of this pseudo-penis appears to be largely androgen-independent, as it develops in the female fetus before the differentiation of the fetal ovary and adrenal gland.

Dolphins are known for their indiscriminate sexual behavior. They engage in sexual activity with both opposite and same genders, as well as masturbate using inanimate objects.
