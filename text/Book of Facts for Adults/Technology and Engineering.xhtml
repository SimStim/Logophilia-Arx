 Non-fiction Trivia
Technology and Engineering

The first computer programmer was Ada Lovelace, who wrote an algorithm for Charles Babbage’s early mechanical general-purpose computer, the Analytical Engine, in the mid-1800s. The engine was never completed and so her program was never tested.

Contrary to popular belief, the QWERTY layout was not designed to slow typists down, but to increase typing speed. Evidence suggests that placing frequently used keys farther apart can actually boost typing speed by encouraging alternation between the hands, aside from addressing the issue of typewriter jamming.

Introduced in June 1980, the IBM 3380 Direct Access Storage Device used film head technology and had a capacity of 2.52 gigabytes (two assemblies, each with two actuators accessing 630 MB). It featured a data transfer rate of 3 MB/s and an average access time of 16 ms. The purchase price ranged from $81,000 to $142,200.

Alan Turing, a key figure in computer science, helped crack the Enigma code during World War II, significantly shortening the war.

On September 9, 1945, during tests of the Mark II Aiken Relay Calculator at Harvard University, a moth was found trapped in Relay #70, Panel F. The operators taped the moth to the computer log, noting it as the “first actual case of bug being found.” They announced they had “debugged” the machine, thus coining the term “debugging” for fixing computer programs.

Log entry of the first computer bug

The first email ever sent was by Ray Tomlinson to himself in 1971, and it was just a random string of letters. SNDMSG was an early email program that initially allowed users on a single TENEX time-sharing computer to send emails to each other. In 1971, Ray Tomlinson extended SNDMSG to send emails to users on other ARPANET-connected computers by appending the recipient’s host name after an “@” sign. Together with READMAIL, SNDMSG became a key component of networked mail on TENEX.

Bluetooth technology is named after a 10th-century Scandinavian king, Harald Bluetooth, known for uniting Denmark and Norway.

After several failed attempts, Orville Wright achieved the first powered flight of a heavier-than-air aircraft, the Wright Flyer, on December 17, 1903, at Kill Devil Hills near Kitty Hawk, North Carolina. The flight lasted 12 seconds, covered 120 feet, and reached a top speed of 6.8 miles per hour.

GPS technology relies on the time signals from at least four satellites to determine the precise location on Earth: three to determine a position on the Earth, and one to adjust for the error in the receiver’s clock.

The world’s first programmable robot, the Unimate, was used in 1961 to handle hot metal at General Motors’ assembly line.

In 1976, Apple co-founders Steve Wozniak and Steve Jobs sold their first pre-assembled computer, the Apple-1. It cost them $250 to build and retailed for $666.66. “As a mathematician, I like repeating digits, and that was what I thought it should be,” Wozniak explained to Bloomberg in 2014.

The Hubble Space Telescope has provided data that helped determine the universe’s age, approximately 13.8 billion years.

The first webcam was used at the University of Cambridge to monitor a coffee pot, ensuring researchers didn’t waste a trip for an empty pot. The 128 × 128 px greyscale camera was connected to the laboratory’s local network through a video capture card fitted on an Acorn Archimedes computer.

Completed in 1945, ENIAC (Electronic Numerical Integrator and Computer) was the first programmable, electronic, general-purpose digital computer. Unlike earlier machines, it combined all these features, was Turing-complete, and could solve various numerical problems through reprogramming. ENIAC was room-sized and consumed as much power as several hundred modern PCs.

The Mars Climate Orbiter, designed to study Mars and relay communications for the Mars Polar Lander and Deep Space probes, failed due to a navigation error from a unit conversion mistake (English to metric). Contact was lost on September 23, 1999, nine months post-launch, and an investigation revealed the spacecraft burned up in Mars’ atmosphere.

The Large Hadron Collider (LHC) at CERN, the world’s largest and most powerful particle accelerator, spans 17 miles (27 km) in circumference.

The Antikythera mechanism is an ancient Greek hand-powered orrery (model of the Solar System) and the oldest known analogue computer. It predicted astronomical positions, eclipses, and tracked the four-year cycle of athletic games like the ancient Olympics. Discovered in 1901, it was among wreckage retrieved from a shipwreck off the coast of the Greek island Antikythera.

The Baghdad Battery refers to three artifacts: a ceramic pot, a copper tube, and an iron rod, discovered in Khujut Rabu, Iraq, in 1936, near ancient Ctesiphon. Dating back to the Parthian or Sasanian periods, its purpose remains unclear. Wilhelm König, then director of the National Museum of Iraq, suggested it was a galvanic cell for electroplating or electrotherapy, but no electroplated objects from that era exist, and archaeologists reject this claim. An alternative theory is that it was used to store sacred scrolls.

China’s Tiangong Space Station is the third space station operated by humans, with a modular design that includes living quarters, labs, and storage, designed to accommodate astronauts for extended periods.

Robots like the da Vinci Surgical System enable surgeons to perform complex operations with precision using small incisions, reducing recovery times and complications.

Omnidirectional treadmills, like the Virtuix Omni, allow users to walk, run, and jump in virtual reality environments by tracking their movements in all directions.

Engineers have developed materials that can repair themselves when damaged, inspired by biological systems like human skin.

Nanotechnology involves manipulating matter on an atomic or molecular scale, leading to applications such as nanomedicine, nanoelectronics, and nanomaterials.

The Stratolaunch Roc is the world’s largest airplane by wingspan, designed to launch rockets into space from high altitude, reducing the cost and complexity of traditional ground-based launches.

Scientists are developing bioluminescent trees that could one day replace traditional streetlights by emitting a natural glow, powered by the energy of the tree itself.

A “Firefly” petunia, genetically engineered to produce a glow

Carbon capture technologies aim to remove carbon dioxide from the atmosphere or emissions, potentially mitigating climate change by storing carbon underground or converting it into useful products.

In 2005, Estonia became the first country to hold legally binding general elections over the Internet with a pilot for municipal elections. The Estonian internet voting system uses the national ID card, which is both a mandatory identity document and a smart card. This card allows secure remote authentication and legally binding digital signatures, supported by Estonia’s public key infrastructure.

3D printers have been adapted for use in space to manufacture tools, spare parts, and even potential habitats using materials available locally, reducing the need for resupply missions.

Smartdust is a system of tiny microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) like sensors and robots that detect light, temperature, vibration, magnetism, or chemicals. Operated wirelessly on a computer network, they are distributed over an area for tasks like sensing via radio-frequency identification. They can offer insights into everything from agriculture to military operations.

In 2015, undergraduate engineering students Seth Robertson and Viet Tran developed an acoustic fire extinguisher as their senior design project at George Mason University. This device disrupts the combustion process, suggesting a potentially safer and more effective method in specific environments.

Metamaterial cloaking involves using metamaterials in invisibility cloaks, manipulating light paths through innovative optical materials. These metamaterials control light propagation, potentially making objects appear invisible by directing specific parts of the light spectrum.

Vertical farming utilizes stacked layers in controlled environments to grow crops indoors, reducing land use, water consumption, and transportation costs while increasing food security in urban areas.

Advances in prosthetic limbs include mind-controlled devices that restore mobility and sensation, blurring the line between human and machine and offering new opportunities for amputees.
