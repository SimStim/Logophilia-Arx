 Non-fiction Trivia
Literature and Language

The longest novel ever written is “In Search of Lost Time” by Marcel Proust, which contains about 1.2 million words.

The shortest story ever written is often attributed to Ernest Hemingway: “For sale: baby shoes, never worn.”

Shakespeare invented over 1,700 words that we use today, including “assassination,” “eyeball,” “generous,” “fashionable,” “luggage,” “lackluster,” and “torture.”

The word “bookkeeper” (and its derivatives) is the only unhyphenated English word with three consecutive double letters.

“The Tale of Genji” by Murasaki Shikibu, written in 11th-century Japan, is widely considered the first novel (defined as an extended work of narrative fiction usually written in prose and published as a book). It is notable for its early depiction of intimate experiences within a narrative form.

According to Ethnologue, an annual reference publication that provides statistics and information on the living languages of the world, more than 7,000 languages are spoken today. However, over 3,000 of these languages (about 40%) are endangered.

The longest word in English is the chemical name for the largest known protein, Titin (a contraction for Titan protein), spanning 189,819 letters and taking almost two hours to pronounce. Whether it qualifies as a word is debated. The longest word published in a dictionary is the chemical name of E. coli TrpA (P0A877), which is 1,909 letters long.

In 2004, Forbes named J.K. Rowling “the first billion-dollar author.” However, Rowling denied being a billionaire in a 2005 interview. By 2012, Forbes concluded that she was no longer a billionaire due to her charitable donations and high UK taxes.

According to Oxford Dictionaries, ‘dreamt’ is the only English word that ends with ‘mt’.

The sentence “The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog” uses every letter in the English alphabet and is called a pangram.

The longest single-word palindrome in the Oxford English Dictionary is the 12-letter onomatopoeic word “tattarrattat,” coined by James Joyce in “Ulysses” (1922) to represent a knock on the door. However, the Guinness Book of Records gives the title to the 11-letter word “detartrated,” the past tense of “detartrate,” a chemical term meaning to remove tartrates.

Mark Twain was born shortly after Halley’s Comet passed by Earth. According to his biographer Albert Bigelow Paine, Twain remarked in 1909, “I came in with Halley’s Comet in 1835. It is coming again next year, and I expect to go out with it.” Twain died on April 21, 1910, one day after the comet’s perihelion, when it emerged from the far side of the sun.

The word “alphabet” comes from the first two letters of the Greek alphabet: alpha and beta.

J. R. R. Tolkien was not only a fantasy writer but also a philologist who invented several languages, including Elvish, for his Middle-earth universe.

“Gadsby” is a 1939 50,000-word novel by Ernest Vincent Wright, notable for being written without using the letter ‘E,’ the most common letter in English. A work that deliberately omits certain letters is known as a lipogram.

The word “robot” was introduced to English by Czech playwright Karel Čapek in his 1920 play “R.U.R.” (Rossum’s Universal Robots). It derives from the Church Slavonic word “robota,” meaning “servitude” or “forced labor,” reflecting the central European system of serfdom where tenants paid rent through labor.

William Shakespeare is estimated to have used a vocabulary of over 20,000 different words in his works.

“Tom Sawyer” is alleged by Mark Twain to be one of the first novels to be written on a typewriter.

In nineteenth-century America, an abbreviation trend akin to modern text shortcuts like LOL and OMG emerged among educated elites, characterized by humorous misspellings and abbreviations. The term “o.k.,” originating from “oll korrect,” a playful misspelling of “all correct,” first appeared in print on March 23, 1839, in the Boston Morning Post. It can be attributed to editor Charles Gordon Greene.

“Dr. Awkward & Olson in Oslo” by Lawrence Levine is a novel that is entirely palindromic.

James Joyce’s “Ulysses” contains a sentence of 4,491 words.

The word “knight” has three silent letters: ‘k,’ ‘g,’ and ‘h.’

Uisge beatha, Scottish Gaelic for ‘water of life’, translates literally as ‘water’ (uisge) and ‘life’ (beatha). Derived from the Latin ‘aqua vitae’, it gradually evolved into the term ‘whisky’ through common usage in Scotland.

Ancient Greeks credited Homer with the epic poems the Iliad and the Odyssey. Modern scholars generally agree that he composed the Iliad (though not as its original creator), likely drawing from oral traditions, and that he at least influenced the composition of the Odyssey.

The word “goodbye” traces back to the Old English term “godbwye,” which directly translates to “God be with you.”

Many languages have been lost to history, leaving behind no written records, so we know little about them. A 2004 estimate suggests that around 90% of currently spoken languages could become extinct by 2050.

The Voynich manuscript is a 15th-century illustrated codex written in an unknown script, dubbed Voynichese. Carbon-dating places its origin between 1404 and 1438, likely in Italy during the Renaissance. Its purpose and authorship remain subjects of debate, with theories ranging from it being a natural or constructed language, a cryptographic puzzle, or possibly a literary or hoax manuscript. Despite various interpretations, the manuscript’s content defies clear understanding, lacking translations and context to confirm its true nature as a factual reference, folklore compendium, or work of speculative fiction like science fantasy or metafiction.

An autological word, also known as a homological word, describes a characteristic it possesses. For instance, “pentasyllabic” has five syllables, “writable” can be written, and “English” is in English. Conversely, a heterological word does not apply to itself, such as “palindrome” not being a palindrome, “monosyllabic” having more than one syllable, “non-hyphenated” being hyphenated, “long” being a short word, and “hyphenated” not having a hyphen.

Papua New Guinea is home to a vast array of languages, with over 800 spoken throughout the country, though more are recognized. It holds the distinction of being the most linguistically diverse nation globally.

The “Mahabharata,” an ancient Indian epic, is the longest poem ever written, with about 100,000 stanzas, 200,000 lines, and 1.8 million words.

New York City’s culture is defined by its immense size and ethnic diversity. With up to 800 languages spoken, it stands as the world’s most linguistically diverse city. Additionally, many influential American cultural movements have originated within its vibrant streets.

Some classic books, like “1984” by George Orwell and “The Catcher in the Rye” by J.D. Salinger, have been banned in various countries at different times.
